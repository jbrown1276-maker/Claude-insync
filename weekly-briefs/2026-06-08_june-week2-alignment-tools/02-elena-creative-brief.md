## 1. Campaign Concept — The Big Idea

**Concept Name: "Now You Have the Words"**

This week's creative lives in a specific emotional territory: the relief of finally having language for something you've been living with but couldn't name.

Practitioners in this audience don't need to be convinced the problem is real. They've been in the sessions where things went sideways. They've been the facilitator who had to improvise because the design intent was never documented. They've been the producer who got the run-of-show the morning of. They know.

What they haven't had is a shared way to describe the gap — and therefore a shared way to close it. The Design to Delivery Alignment Checklist gives them both. The creative concept isn't "here's a tool." It's "here's the word for the thing you've been trying to explain — and here's how to fix it."

Every piece this week should create a recognition moment: *that's the thing. That's exactly the thing.* The checklist is the resolution. The webinar is where the resolution deepens.

**What this creative is doing this week:**
- Naming the gap with precision so practitioners feel seen
- Handing over a practical tool with confidence — not hedging, not qualifying
- Creating a clean, frictionless path from recognition to download to registration
- Establishing Karen Vieth's video as a peer voice, not a vendor voice

**What this creative is NOT doing this week:**
- Explaining what alignment is conceptually — practitioners know
- Selling the webinar before the checklist has earned the right
- Producing anything aspirational, inspirational, or vision-led
- Making this feel like a product launch with fanfare

## 2. Creative Pillars

**Pillar 1: Precision Over Warmth**
The creative earns emotional resonance through accuracy, not through softness. When we name the exact moment a facilitator realizes they don't know what an activity is supposed to prove, we create more warmth than any stock photo of smiling colleagues in a meeting room. Devon: this means diagrams and specificity in visuals, not lifestyle imagery. Sarah: this means naming the scenario before offering the solution.

*How it shows up:*
- In copy: specific scenarios, specific roles, specific consequences — then the tool
- In design: clean systems visuals, before/after states, annotated diagrams
- In video: real workflow language, not produced motivational tone

**Pillar 2: The Handoff as the Hero**
The checklist is not a product. It is a handoff between people who care about the same outcome. Every piece should frame it as a shared object — something the designer hands to the facilitator, something the facilitator shares with the producer. Devon: the visual metaphor is a document being passed between hands, not a hero asset on a pedestal. Sarah: the copy should feel transactional in the best sense — here, take this, use it this week.

*How it shows up:*
- In copy: "run it on one program this week" — action language, not aspiration language
- In design: workflow diagrams showing people at different stages of the same process
- In email: the checklist as the opening gift, the webinar as the next conversation

**Pillar 3: Peer Credibility**
Karen Vieth's video is the clearest expression of this pillar, but it should run through everything. This campaign does not speak from on high. It speaks from beside. InSync has seen this problem across hundreds of programs. The content this week reflects that — not as expertise being dispensed, but as pattern recognition being shared.

*How it shows up:*
- In copy: "in our work with complex virtual programs, one of the first patterns we see is…" — Jennifer's register in Blog 2 is the model
- In design: no authority imagery — practitioners working together, annotating documents, running sessions
- In video: Karen speaking directly, simply, without production polish that creates distance

**Pillar 4: Momentum Without Pressure**
The checklist download and the webinar registration are two stops on the same journey. The creative should make the path feel natural — not urgent, not pushy. Devon: visual continuity between checklist assets and webinar promotion assets. Sarah: write the webinar CTA as a continuation, not a pivot.

*How it shows up:*
- In copy: "the checklist is the start; the webinar is where the practice deepens"
- In design: matching visual language across checklist and webinar assets so they read as a system
- In CTAs: checklist CTA is active ("download and run it this week"); webinar CTA is relational ("bring your questions live")

## 3. Visual Direction (for Devon)

**Color Application**

- InSync Blue (#032b43): authority anchor — headers, primary backgrounds, weight moments
- Cool Blue (#0883cf): action color — every CTA button, every link, interactive elements only
- Light Blue (#00b9f5): energy accent — checklist callout boxes, section dividers, one highlight moment per asset
- Golden Honey (#eda113): the checklist's color — callout boxes in Blog 2, checklist preview graphic, "new this week" badge. Warm, immediate, distinct from the blues.
- White space is structural, not decorative. Every visual decision that adds weight without adding meaning should be cut.

**Typography**

- Montserrat Bold: all headlines, no exceptions
- Montserrat Medium: subheads and checklist sections
- Montserrat Regular: body copy (not Light — content is practical, weight should feel solid)
- Hierarchy: headline states the scenario or tool → subhead names the specific element → body explains. Three levels maximum.

**Imagery**

Scenario: a team at the handoff moment — a designer and facilitator sitting across a document together, a producer reviewing an annotated run-of-show, someone with a printed and marked-up checklist. Natural light, home office or corporate, slightly informal.

Never shoot: stock smiles in a training room, a presenter at a whiteboard with an audience, anyone using a tablet or phone to represent "learning."

For social and video thumbnails: real faces in working contexts. Karen Vieth's video should feel like a colleague's Zoom call, not a produced interview.

**Iconography**

Core motif: the handoff — an arrow moving between Design → Delivery, a document with two sets of hands, a checklist item being marked. Line-weight, not filled.

Drift vs. alignment visual: two parallel lines diverging (drift) vs. two lines converging on a shared point (alignment). Whiteboard-simple. Not motion graphics.

Industry vertical icons: minimal, functional — cross (healthcare), circuit (tech), hard hat (manufacturing). One idea per icon, consistent pixel weight.

**Layout Principles**

- Email: single column, InSync Blue header, Golden Honey callout for checklist, Cool Blue CTA buttons, no sidebars
- Newsletter: single column — checklist graphic leads, then video thumbnails, then webinar CTA
- LinkedIn posts: image-first, one line of text maximum on image

**What to Avoid**

No split-screen before/after layouts that look like software comparison ads. No checklist illustrations that look like app UI mockups — show it as a physical document. No leadership imagery: no silhouettes, no summit metaphors, no people looking at horizons.

## 4. Tone of Voice (for Sarah)

**Governing principle:** Write like Jennifer wrote the blog — as someone who has seen the pattern, names it precisely, and hands over the thing that fixes it.

**What it sounds like:**

*Email #2:* "The Design to Delivery Alignment Checklist is here. It's one page — a handoff checklist, a run-of-show outline, and a design risk scan. Run it on one program before your next launch and see what it surfaces. Then come to the webinar and bring what you found."

*LinkedIn Post 1:* "Most delivery problems were solvable before the session began. The design intent wasn't documented. The producer cue wasn't clear. The facilitator knew what to do — but not why it mattered. The Design to Delivery Alignment Checklist fixes the handoff before it becomes a problem."

*Newsletter:* "This week, the checklist arrives. One page. Three sections. The questions your design and delivery team should be answering together before any program launches."

*LinkedIn Newsletter Issue 1:* "The most common delivery problem isn't a facilitation problem or a production problem. It's a documentation problem. When design intent doesn't travel with the materials, every facilitator interprets the session differently."

**What to avoid:**

Never write "we're excited to share." Never write "in today's fast-paced L&D environment." No rhetorical manipulation questions. No "game-changer," "unlock," or "transform."

Wrong: "Empower your facilitation team with the tools they need to drive alignment across your programs."
Right: "Give your facilitator the one thing they're usually missing before delivery begins: the designer's intent."

The test: does this sound like something a smart, experienced practitioner would say to a colleague? If it sounds like marketing, rewrite it.

## 5. Creative Guardrails

**What fits:**
- Opening with the scenario — the specific moment the problem shows up
- Naming roles by title (facilitator, producer, instructional designer), not abstraction
- Data points used to underscore cost of the problem, not make a sales argument
- Karen Vieth's name used specifically — never "InSync expert"
- The checklist described by all three components every time: handoff checklist, run-of-show outline, design risk scan
- The webinar described by what you leave with: Designer Self-Assessment, action plan, real case stories

**What does not fit:**
- Anything requiring the audience to aspire — they want to be seen where they are
- Testimonials or social proof — the tool speaks for itself
- References to Week 1 as a prerequisite — this week's content must stand alone
- LinkedIn Newsletter framed as "tips" or "best practices" — it's analysis, not a listicle

**The CTA Rule:**
Checklist CTA appears first in every piece. Webinar CTA closes every piece. They never swap. If a piece carries only one CTA, it carries the checklist.

Webinar CTA copy must always name what the practitioner gets: "Leave with the Designer Self-Assessment, a case story walk-through, and a 30-day action plan." Never "join us to learn about alignment."

## 6. Key Messages in Creative Language

**Tier 1 — The Checklist Launch**
Strategic message: The tool is here. Run it on one program this week.
Creative approach: Make arrival feel like relief, not fanfare. Devon: checklist graphic is the hero visual — clear, legible, real. Sarah: "here it is, here's what it does, here's what to do with it" — three sentences maximum for the lead moment.

**Tier 2 — The Proof**
Strategic message: 30% less rework, 25% faster time-to-competency. When design intent travels, facilitators connect it to learner WIIFM.
Creative approach: Data underscore cost of the problem, not sell the solution. Devon: no data visualization needed — numbers live in body copy. Sarah: Brandon Hall figure once, early, to establish cost. 25%/30% numbers in newsletter and email body. Lead with scenarios, let data confirm.

**Tier 3 — The Videos**
Strategic message: Video #1 explains the system. Video #2 — Karen Vieth — makes it human.
Creative approach: Devon: Video #1 thumbnail = before/after workflow. Video #2 thumbnail = Karen direct to camera with single text overlay. Sarah: one sentence per video introduction that earns the click — not a summary, a reason to watch.

**Tier 4 — The Webinar**
Strategic message: Apply the checklist live, with real case stories and an action plan.
Creative approach: Devon: reuse checklist visual language in webinar promotion for continuity. Sarah: "Bring your checklist. Leave with your action plan." Registration feels like accepting a colleague's invitation.

## 7. Audience Insight — The North Star

**The insight:** This audience doesn't need to be persuaded alignment matters. They need to feel someone has done the hard work of turning that knowledge into something usable on Monday.

**The creative opportunity:** The recognition moment is not "InSync gets it" — it's "someone finally said it out loud, and they handed me the fix."

**The north star question Devon and Sarah ask before finalizing any decision:**
*Does this make a practitioner feel like we built this for them specifically — or does it make them feel like we're marketing at them?*

If the answer is the latter, it goes back.
