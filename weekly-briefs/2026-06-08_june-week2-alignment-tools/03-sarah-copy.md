Most delivery teams go into a program with materials. The checklist makes sure they also go in with shared understanding — and a named risk level before the first cohort runs.

## 6. LinkedIn Newsletter — Issue 1

**Title:** Why Programs Fail in Delivery When Design Lacks Operational Clarity

---

**Full Copy**

The most expensive delivery problems are usually documentation problems.

Not facilitation problems. Not platform problems. Not even design problems, technically. The design was done. The materials were ready. But the design intent didn't travel with them — and by the time the delivery team was in prep week, there was no shared document that explained what the program was supposed to prove, which activities were non-negotiable, or where the producer needed to be actively watching.

So the team improvised. And improvisation at scale is expensive.

**The pattern is consistent**

Across programs and industries — technology implementations, healthcare compliance rollouts, manufacturing onboarding, financial services leadership development — the breakdown follows the same sequence.

Design finishes. Delivery inherits. The handoff happens informally, if at all. The run-of-show varies by facilitator. The design risk scan doesn't exist. And by the time a delivery team is running the twelfth cohort of a program, the experience has drifted from what the designer built — gradually, without anyone deciding that was acceptable.

This is not a talent problem. The facilitators are skilled. The producers know their platforms. The instructional designers built a solid program. The problem is that the intent behind the design is living in someone's head, not in a shared document that the delivery team can reference, question, and build on.

When design intent doesn't travel, delivery teams fill the gap with interpretation. Interpretation produces variation. Variation undermines the consistency that makes live learning scale.

**What the data reflects**

Teams with documented handoff standards — shared run-of-show practices, explicit design intent documentation, pre-delivery risk scans — consistently report fewer revision cycles, cleaner handoffs, and faster learner application across cohorts.

Those improvements are not primarily about the quality of the individual facilitator or producer. They are about the quality of the handoff. When the delivery team understands what the program is supposed to prove, they make better decisions in the room. They know what to protect, what can flex, and where the learning outcome depends on a specific kind of participation.

Without that clarity, every delivery is a fresh interpretation.

**The operational response**

InSync has developed the Design to Delivery Alignment Checklist specifically to address this gap — a one-page tool that gives every program a shared standard before the delivery team walks into the room.

It has three sections. The Handoff Checklist confirms that the facilitator guide communicates design intent — not just structure — and that every role has a checklist for before, during, and after the program. The Run-of-Show Outline creates a shared execution standard: transition language at every handoff point, a named owner for every interaction, and a confirmed rehearsal date before the first session runs. The Design Risk Scan is the pre-launch gate — six yes/no questions designed to surface what's been assumed rather than documented. The scan ends with a program risk level: Low means ready to deliver. Medium means specific gaps must be resolved before launch. High means do not launch.

That final question in the risk scan is the one most leadership teams should be asking before every major rollout: *Could someone new deliver this reliably with what is currently documented?* In most programs, the honest answer is no — and that gap is what the checklist is built to close.

In a recent technology program rollout, using a structured handoff document of this kind reduced delivery variation across regional cohorts and cut delivery prep escalations by removing the most common source of last-minute fixes: assumptions that had never been written down.

The checklist is available to download this week. Run it on one program before your next launch.
