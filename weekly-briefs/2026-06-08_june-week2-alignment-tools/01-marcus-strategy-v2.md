## 2. Target Audience Analysis

**Segment 1: Practitioners (Primary Activation Target)**

Roles: Facilitators, Instructional Designers, Producers, Learning Operations Managers

Psychographic depth: These are practitioners who have been doing this work long enough to know what good looks like — and to feel the daily friction when it doesn't happen. They are not looking for someone to explain the problem to them. They already know. What they are hungry for is a tool they can bring to their team on Monday morning without having to write it themselves.

Their frustration is specific: they can see exactly where the breakdown happened — the unclear producer cue, the facilitator who had to improvise because the design intent was never documented — and they are tired of doing triage when they know upstream alignment would have prevented it. They want to feel competent, not heroic.

What will resonate: Immediate usability. Specificity over abstraction. Practical language that names the scenarios they recognize. Proof that InSync has done the hard thinking so they don't have to start from scratch. The checklist itself is the message — everything else frames why it matters and what to do next.

What will not resonate: Theory. Process language. Anything that positions alignment as a cultural initiative or leadership conversation rather than a practical operational tool. They have heard "alignment matters" before. This week, they need to see it in a usable format.

Activation goal: Download the checklist. Register for the webinar to apply it in a live session.

**Segment 2: L&D Leadership (Secondary Amplification Target)**

Roles: CLOs, Directors of Learning Operations, VPs of L&D, Senior Learning Leaders

Psychographic depth: Leaders are paying attention to this campaign because Week 1 framed the problem in terms they recognize. But they engage differently. They are not the ones filling in the checklist. They are the ones who need to know whether their teams have the tools to be consistent at scale. The 25% time-to-competency improvement and 30% rework reduction figures speak directly to their accountability.

What will resonate: Evidence of operational impact. The story that one shared tool reduces variation across regions, programs, and cohorts. The LinkedIn Newsletter (Issue 1) is their primary touchpoint this week — thought-leadership positioning aimed at the decision-making layer, not the execution layer.

What will not resonate: Tool-level detail. Step-by-step walkthroughs of the checklist. Content that requires them to imagine themselves doing the work rather than overseeing it.

Amplification goal: Engage with LinkedIn Newsletter Issue 1. Share the checklist with their teams. Register for or champion webinar attendance within their organizations.

**Segment prioritization:** Practitioners are the primary activation target this week. The checklist is for them. The webinar is for them. All primary content — blog, email, weekly newsletter, LinkedIn posts — should speak to practitioners first. The LinkedIn Newsletter is the one piece intentionally scoped for leaders.

## 3. Messaging Pillars and Hierarchy

**The One Thing:**
The tool is here. One alignment step prevents a week of delivery fixes — and the webinar is where you use it live.

This week's single most important message is the arrival of the checklist. Everything else — the videos, the blog, the newsletter — exists to demonstrate its value and move practitioners toward download and webinar registration.

**Tier 1 — Lead Message (Checklist Launch)**
*Strategic function: Announce the primary lead magnet and establish its immediate usability.*

"The Design to Delivery Alignment Checklist is here — a one-page handoff checklist, run-of-show outline, and design risk scan. Run it on one program this week."

This message should lead every piece of content. It is the relief moment. Practitioners who read Week 1 and thought "we need this" are now being handed exactly what they were looking for.

**Tier 2 — Proof Message (Why It Works)**
*Strategic function: Validate the tool's effectiveness and the cost of not using it.*

"Teams with documented handoff standards report 30% less rework and 25% faster time-to-competency. When design intent is explicit, facilitators can tie it to learner WIIFM — which is where durable behavior change begins."

This message does the rational work. The $13.5M Brandon Hall figure and the 30–40% engagement increase from technical producers are supporting data — use them to underscore the cost of the problem, not just the benefit of the solution.

**Tier 3 — Application Message (The Videos)**
*Strategic function: Show the checklist working, not just describe it.*

"Microlearning Video #1 shows what changes when design is explicit instead of assumed. Microlearning Video #2 — Karen Vieth, two minutes — shows how understanding design intent lets facilitators connect the session to what learners actually need."

Video #2 is a trust builder. Karen Vieth's name and the VLF practitioner lens signal that this is peer-to-peer insight, not a vendor pitch. Treat the two videos as a sequence: Video #1 explains the system, Video #2 makes it human.

**Tier 4 — Conversion Message (Webinar)**
*Strategic function: Move practitioners from "I have the tool" to "I want to apply it with InSync."*

"The practitioner webinar is where the checklist gets used live — real case stories, shared workflows, and a Designer Self-Assessment and action plan you leave with."

The webinar CTA should not overshadow the checklist announcement. This week, the checklist earns trust. The webinar is the next step for practitioners who are ready to go deeper. Present it as natural momentum, not a pivot.

**What to avoid this week:**
Do not lead with the webinar. Leading with a registration ask before delivering the tool undercuts the trust the checklist is designed to build. Practitioners need to receive something of value before they are asked to commit time. Keep the webinar as a closing call to action on every piece — not an opener.

## 5. Tone and Positioning Guardrails

**What this campaign must sound like:**

Practical relief. The practitioner reading this content has been doing delivery prep under pressure. The tone should feel like a colleague handing them something that actually works — not a vendor announcing a product launch. Warm, direct, specific.

Concrete over abstract, always. Every sentence that could be vague should be made specific. Not "alignment improves delivery" — "when the facilitator knows which activity matters most and why, they make better pacing decisions in the room." Show the scenario, name the role, describe what changes.

Peer credibility, not authority. Karen Vieth's video works because it is practitioner-to-practitioner. The rest of the content should carry that register. InSync knows what it's talking about, but it earns trust by demonstrating understanding of practitioners' actual workday, not by citing credentials.

**Concrete examples of on-brand voice:**

- "Before the session begins, check one thing: does your delivery team understand what each activity is supposed to prove?" (Practical, direct, scenario-grounded)
- "The checklist doesn't add a layer of administration. It removes a week of delivery fixes." (Clear tradeoff, zero jargon)
- "Karen Vieth in two minutes: what happens when a facilitator finally knows the design intent behind a session." (Human, specific, anticipatory)

**What to avoid:**

- "Comprehensive framework" — sounds like a consultant selling a methodology, not a practitioner offering a tool
- "Empower your teams" — empty aspirational language; say what the tool actually does instead
- "Drive alignment" — vague; name the specific handoff or decision point instead
- Opening any piece with the webinar — this week, the checklist earns trust first
- Any framing that positions the problem as a leadership culture issue rather than a practical operational gap — practitioners are not in a position to fix culture; they can use a checklist

## 8. Recommended Creative Direction Starting Points

These are strategic springboards for Elena. They identify opportunities and the emotional register each should hit. They are not creative concepts.

**Springboard 1: "The Gap Nobody Names"**
Strategic opportunity: Practitioners know something is missing in the handoff, but there is rarely shared language for it. The checklist gives the gap a name and a solution simultaneously. Creative should lean into that moment of recognition — the feeling of "this is exactly what we needed and didn't know how to ask for."
Emotional register: Clarity after fog. Relief, not triumph. The satisfaction of naming something that has been unnamed.

**Springboard 2: "Silence Before the Session"**
Strategic opportunity: The most powerful scene in the brief is the one described in Blog 2: everything looked ready, but the gaps didn't appear until the session started. There is a moment — the quiet before a live delivery — where the delivery team either has what they need or is about to improvise. Creative can make that moment visceral.
Emotional register: Tension resolved by preparation. The practitioner who has the checklist can walk into that room differently than the one who doesn't.

**Springboard 3: "Shared Language"**
Strategic opportunity: The checklist is described as "a shared language between designers, facilitators, and producers — not a compliance document." This distinction matters. Teams that use the checklist are not checking boxes — they are getting on the same page before the learner arrives. Creative should make that collaborative moment feel achievable, not bureaucratic.
Emotional register: Belonging. The sense of a team that moves together rather than improvising in parallel. Practical warmth.

**Springboard 4: "Karen's Two Minutes"**
Strategic opportunity: Video #2 is a short, practitioner-to-practitioner piece led by Karen Vieth. This is an opportunity to treat a brief video as a moment of genuine peer insight rather than a content asset. The WIIFM framing — when facilitators understand design intent, they can connect the session to what learners actually need — is the emotional core of this springboard.
Emotional register: Recognition. The moment a facilitator sees that their role is not just to deliver the session but to translate the design intent for the learner in front of them.
