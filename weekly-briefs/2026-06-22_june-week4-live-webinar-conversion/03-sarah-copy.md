## 2. LinkedIn Newsletter — Issue 2

**Title: "Where Consistency Breaks — And Why Coaching Facilitators Won't Fix It"**

*Standalone thought leadership for L&D leaders. ~810 words. Does not mention the webinar or replay.*

---

There's a pattern that shows up in L&D organizations of every size and sector. A program launches. Feedback from the first cohort is solid. By cohort three or four, something has drifted. Different facilitators are running different sessions. Learner experience varies by region. The L&D team adds a QA layer, refreshes the facilitator guide, maybe brings in an outside observer. Scores stabilize — but they never quite reach what was expected when the program was first designed.

The standard diagnosis is a delivery problem. And so the intervention stays at the delivery level: more facilitator preparation, more observation, more coaching after the fact.

The evidence points somewhere else.

**The gap is upstream.**

Consider what we know: 98% of organizations use virtual instructor-led training. Only 21% report achieving the highest levels of success. That's not a gap you close by coaching facilitators harder. That's a structural gap — and its origin is in how programs are built before the first session runs.

When a facilitator interprets a session differently than the designer intended, it's rarely because the facilitator isn't skilled. It's because the design didn't travel with the materials. The session deck arrived. The intent didn't. There was no shared language between the person who built the program and the person delivering it — so each facilitator filled in the gaps with their own judgment.

This is a design capability problem. The ceiling on a session's consistency was set during design, long before a facilitator ever logs in.

**What design capability means at scale**

It's easy to assume that if your team is producing programs, they have what they need. But there's a meaningful difference between the ability to build a course and the ability to build a course that holds up across a hundred different facilitation contexts, three time zones, and a hybrid room configuration you didn't fully anticipate.

The skills that produce consistency at scale are specific ones: designing with delivery handoff in mind; anticipating hybrid complexity before it becomes a delivery incident; collaborating across the designer-facilitator-producer boundary rather than throwing materials over the wall; evaluating programs for actual behavior change, not completion rates.

These skills live in four domains — instructional design fundamentals, virtual and hybrid design, collaboration and handoff, and performance and application. When designers have uneven proficiency across those domains, the inconsistency leaders observe in delivery often traces directly to the programs those designers built.

Teams with documented handoff standards report 30% less rework and 25% faster time to competency. The handoff standard is a design skill. It gets built or overlooked during design — long before a facilitator logs in.

**What leaders can practically do**

The organizations that close this gap do it by treating design capability as a measurable, improvable function — not a credential you either have or don't.

Two things, practically:

First, give your designers a way to see where they actually are. Not against a generic competency framework, but against the specific skills that determine whether a virtual or hybrid program will hold up in delivery. Skills like: selecting the right modality for the learning objective. Designing for the full learning ecosystem — not just the session. Anticipating hybrid delivery needs before they become incidents. Designing with evaluation built in from day one, not added at the end.

Second, create the structural condition that lets design intent travel across the design-delivery boundary. This is the collaboration layer — the point where what the designer built gets transferred to the person running it. When that transfer is documented and deliberate, facilitator consistency improves. When it's assumed, you get drift. Including a producer who understands the purpose — not just the mechanics — of each interaction point can increase learner engagement scores by 30–40%. That's not a delivery outcome. It's a design decision made upstream.

**The leverage point**

Most delivery inconsistency is downstream of a design decision already made — or not made. By the time a facilitator runs a session, the ceiling on that session's consistency was set weeks earlier.

Leaders who understand this stop looking for the fix at the moment of delivery. They start looking at what their designers can actually do, what's being handed off when a program ships, and where the gaps between design intent and delivery reality are costing programs most.

That's a more useful diagnostic than another round of facilitator coaching. And it's a solvable problem — but only if you look for it in the right place.
