## 1. Campaign Concept — The Big Idea

**Concept Name: "The Proof Becomes Practice"**

Weeks 1 through 3 built the case. Week 1 named the problem. Week 2 handed over the tool. Week 3 showed the pattern of improvement. This week, all of that becomes real in a live room — and then it becomes something the audience can carry forward.

"The Proof Becomes Practice" has two phases:

**Phase 1 — Pre-webinar (arrival):** The live session is where June's argument stops being theory. It's a room full of people who already understand why this matters, working through real programs with practitioners who made this shift. Day-of creative should feel like the room is finally full — warm, specific, earned.

**Phase 2 — Post-webinar (momentum):** The risk after any strong event is the post-event dip — the moment when inspiration meets Monday morning. The creative's job is to give the audience one concrete, specific next step before that dip sets in. Not "here's everything you could do." One step. Specific to where they are.

**What the creative must make the audience feel:**
- Pre-webinar: "This is the room I've been waiting to be in."
- Post-webinar: "I know exactly what I'm doing next."

**What the creative is NOT doing this week:**
- Not re-explaining the alignment model (Week 1's job)
- Not re-launching the checklist (Week 2's job)
- Not presenting new evidence (Week 3's job)
- Not making a hard sell on the audit — the audit is offered as a natural next step for teams ready to apply the framework to specific programs

## 2. Creative Pillars

### Pillar 1 — Arrival
The live session is the destination the entire month pointed toward. Every pre-webinar touchpoint should feel like the moment before something important begins — specific, warm, and clear on what's about to happen. Not hype. Readiness.

- **Blog 4:** Month synthesis has its own arrival — "here is what June proved." Infographic carries it.
- **Email #4:** One clear paragraph, one prominent link. No decoration.
- **Newsletter (day-of):** Short and energetic. The link is the focus.
- **LinkedIn Post 1:** Countdown energy, but specific — three things you will practice and leave with.

### Pillar 2 — Acknowledgment
The post-webinar creative leads with recognition before it asks for anything. The audience showed up. They did the work. That deserves a sentence — not a paragraph, not a page, but a real acknowledgment before the next CTA appears.

- **Email #5:** Warm open. Genuine. No "we hope you enjoyed." See CTA rule in Section 5.
- **Newsletter (post-webinar):** Thank attendees. Acknowledge the quality of the community. Then link.
- **LinkedIn Post 2:** Replay announcement, but opens with one sharp takeaway — evidence you were in the room, not just managing the send.

### Pillar 3 — Specificity Over Scale
The post-event dip is solved by specificity, not inspiration. "Apply the framework" is not a next step. "Take the Designer Self-Assessment on one program this week" is a next step. Every post-webinar CTA should be as concrete as possible. One program. One team. This week.

- **Blog 4:** Three leader takeaways that are genuinely actionable next week — not frameworks, actions.
- **Vertical-specific "start here" paths:** tech, manufacturing, healthcare, energy, financial services — each with a concrete first step for that context.

### Pillar 4 — Completion Without Closure
June ends, but the alignment work doesn't. Blog 4 and LinkedIn Newsletter Issue 2 both close the month while pointing forward. The feeling is: "We proved the pattern. Now it's yours to apply." Not "this campaign is over." Not "there's still more to learn." Completion — and forward momentum.

## 4. Tone of Voice (for Sarah)

### Governing Principle

Write like someone who was in the room — and who genuinely believes what they saw.

Pre-webinar: specific, clear, warm without being effusive. The audience has been reading this content for four weeks. They know the argument. They don't need it restated. They need to know what's about to happen and why it's worth showing up for.

Post-webinar: acknowledgment first, then practical. The post-event dip is real. The creative's job is to name the next step before the audience loses momentum. One step. Concrete. Not a menu.

---

### What It Sounds Like

**Pre-webinar (day-of):**
> "Today's the day. Design to Delivery Sync is a live practitioner session — case stories from people who made this shift, workflows you'll apply to a program you bring, and the Designer Self-Assessment to carry it forward. Join us at [time]."

Not: "We're so excited to welcome you to today's event!" — corporate and imprecise.

**Post-webinar acknowledgment (Zone 1):**
> "You showed up. The session ran the way it was built to run — practitioners who already understood the problem, working through programs that actually mattered to them. That's a harder room to build than it looks."

Not: "We hope you found today's session valuable." — evaluative and distancing.

**Post-webinar CTA (Zone 2):**
> "The Designer Self-Assessment is the next step. Take it on one program. See where your current design practice holds up against delivery — and where it doesn't. That's a 30-minute investment that tells you more than a cohort of observation data."

Not: "Download the assessment today and transform your design practice!" — generic and oversold.

**Month close (Blog 4, LinkedIn Newsletter):**
> "June established a pattern. Design alignment creates facilitator consistency. Facilitator consistency protects producer stability. Producer stability protects learner practice quality. That pattern doesn't reverse itself when the webinar ends. It applies to the next program your team builds."

Not: "What an incredible month it's been!" — recap energy, no forward motion.

---

### What to Avoid

- "We hope you enjoyed the session" — generic and surveys-by-proxy
- "Don't miss" — countdown pressure, off-brand
- "Framework" as a standalone noun — always attach a specific outcome
- "Transformative," "game-changing," "innovative" — per brand guidelines, never
- "Best-in-class," "synergy," "leverage" (as verb), "value-add" — brand never-say list
- Any implication that practitioners should have been doing this differently — the frame is systemic, not evaluative
- Replay framed as consolation — replay is equal-value access, not second-best

## 5. Creative Guardrails

### The CTA Rule — Week 4

**PRE-WEBINAR: Single CTA only.**
The pre-webinar CTA is "Attend Live" or "Register." Nothing else. Do not add secondary CTAs to pre-webinar assets. The audience should have one decision to make.

**POST-WEBINAR: Two zones, different rules.**

*Zone 1 — Acknowledgment (top of each post-webinar asset):*
No CTA in Zone 1. The opening of every post-webinar email, newsletter, and relevant LinkedIn post is acknowledgment-only. One to three sentences. Genuine. No button. No link. Just recognition.

*Zone 2 — Action sequence (below Zone 1):*
CTAs appear in this order and no other:
1. Download the *Virtual Learning is Real Learning* Ebook — Cool Blue button (primary, post-webinar)
2. Designer Self-Assessment — text link (secondary)
3. Design to Delivery Alignment Checklist — text link (tertiary)
4. Book an Alignment Audit — Ocean Blue text link (readiness-framed, not urgency-framed)

The audit CTA must always be framed as: "for teams ready to identify where design-to-delivery gaps are costing specific programs most" — not as a sales offer, not as a time-limited opportunity.

**BLOG 4:**
Blog 4 is a synthesis piece for leaders and practitioners. CTAs appear only at the close of the piece, in this order: Watch the Replay (primary), Book an Audit (secondary), Download the Ebook (tertiary). Do not embed CTAs mid-article.

**LINKEDIN NEWSLETTER ISSUE 2:**

## 6. Key Messages in Creative Language

### Tier 1 — The Completion Statement (pre-webinar)
**Strategic message:** This is the live version of everything June built toward.
**Creative approach:** Make the session feel earned, not sold. The audience already knows the argument. Give them specifics: case stories from practitioners who made this shift, workflows applied to a real program they bring, the Designer Self-Assessment to leave with. The creative translates abstraction into "here is what you will actually do for 90 minutes."

### Tier 2 — The Acknowledgment (post-webinar, Zone 1)
**Strategic message:** You showed up. Here is what to do with what you took from the session.
**Creative approach:** Lead with recognition that feels specific — not "we hope you enjoyed it" but "here is what the room looked like from where we were standing." This is the moment where the brand earns trust. The acknowledgment precedes every CTA in every post-webinar asset.

### Tier 3 — The Application Sequence (post-webinar, Zone 2)
**Strategic message:** Framework from the session. Tool from the self-assessment. Application from the audit. One step at a time.
**Creative approach:** The application sequence solves the post-event dip by making the next step specific. Sarah writes each CTA with a concrete outcome attached — not "learn more" but "benchmark your design practice against the 18 skills that most directly affect delivery consistency." The Designer Self-Assessment is the practitioner next step. The audit is for teams ready to apply the framework to specific programs.

**Designer Self-Assessment specifics for Sarah:** The assessment covers 18 skills across four domains. Domain 1 — Instructional Design Fundamentals (needs assessment, learning objectives, modality selection, evaluation design). Domain 2 — Virtual and Hybrid Design (hybrid complexity, platform-independent design, virtual engagement design, multi-modal sequencing). Domain 3 — Collaboration and Handoff (producer-designer collaboration, facilitator guide clarity, run-of-show construction, design documentation). Domain 4 — Performance and Application (behavior change design, spaced practice, application transfer, job aid integration, performance support). Available at: https://www.insynctraining.com/answer-clear/design-skills-self-assessment. Sarah writes about this as "a benchmark against the 18 skills that most directly affect whether your programs hold up in delivery" — not as a generic assessment.

### Tier 4 — The Month Close (Blog 4 and LinkedIn Newsletter Issue 2)
**Strategic message:** June's alignment model doesn't end here. It starts here — with one program, one team, one specific gap identified and addressed.
**Creative approach:** Blog 4 synthesizes the arc and points forward. LinkedIn Newsletter Issue 2 stands alone as thought leadership — it does not reference the webinar. Both pieces close with the feeling of completion that leads somewhere, not completion that ends something.

### C. LinkedIn Newsletter — Issue 2

**Creative role:** Standalone thought leadership for the LinkedIn leadership audience. This piece is NOT about the webinar. It is not a webinar recap. It stands alone as an intellectual contribution to the L&D leadership conversation.

**Title:** "Where Consistency Breaks — And Why Coaching Facilitators Won't Fix It"

**Thought leadership angle:** Most L&D leaders diagnose delivery inconsistency at the delivery level — through facilitator coaching, QA observation, and post-session feedback. The argument here is that the root cause is upstream: when design teams lack the skills to build handoff-ready programs, delivery intervention produces temporary stabilization, not consistent improvement. The fix requires leaders to look at what their designers can actually do — before the program reaches a facilitator.

**Structure for Sarah:**
- Open with the pattern leaders recognize: programs that drift by cohort three or four despite facilitator coaching. Name this without judgment — leaders have already tried the obvious fix.
- The upstream reframe: delivery inconsistency is downstream of a design decision already made. The ceiling on a session's consistency was set during design.
- What design capability actually means at scale: not producing content, but building programs that travel — that hold up when a facilitator fills in the gaps with judgment. The 18-skill self-assessment framing fits here: specific skills (handoff documentation, hybrid anticipation, evaluation design) are what determine whether a program holds across regions.
- What leaders can practically do: (1) give designers a way to see where they are against specific skills; (2) create the structural condition that transfers design intent across the design-delivery boundary.
- Close: the leverage point is upstream. Most delivery inconsistency is downstream of a design decision already made. Leaders who understand this look at what their designers can do — not at what their facilitators are doing.
- Closing paragraph — CTAs in this order only: Designer Self-Assessment link → Alignment Audit booking → Alignment Checklist download. No ebook CTA.

**Proof point to use (Sarah):** 98% of organizations use VILT; only 21% achieve the highest levels of success. Teams with documented handoff standards report 30% less rework and 25% faster time to competency. The 30–40% engagement score increase from dedicated technical producers (from Cindy Foster's source content) can appear as supporting evidence if it fits the argument.

**Visual direction (Devon):**
- Article header image: simple diagram showing the upstream → downstream chain — design decisions flowing into delivery outcomes. InSync Blue and Light Blue (semantic use: after state). Not a marketing graphic — a conceptual diagram.
- If a pull quote visual is used: one, Golden Honey treatment, the sharpest claim from the piece (e.g., "The ceiling on a session's consistency was set during design, long before a facilitator ever logs in.")
- Layout: LinkedIn article format — clean, professional, one image at top or within the body

**Tone (Sarah):** This is the most intellectually demanding register of the week. Leader-facing, not practitioner-facing. The argument should feel corrective — not "here is what you should do" but "here is where the standard diagnosis goes wrong." Confident, grounded in evidence, no brand language that sounds like a pitch.

**What to avoid in Issue 2:**
- Any mention of the webinar or replay
- Practitioner-to-practitioner language
