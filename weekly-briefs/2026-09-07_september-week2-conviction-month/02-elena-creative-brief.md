## 1. Campaign Concept — The Big Idea

**Concept name: "Exactly What It Shows."**

Week 1 put the research on the record. Week 2 states exactly what the record shows — no more, and pointedly no less. The word doing the work is *exact*. Last week's word was *proves*; this week narrows from "here is the evidence" to "here is how evidence is properly claimed." The concept lives in one behavior the whole week performs: state a real number, state the condition that produced it, state the limit of what it proves, and let all three stand together as one confident object.

Inside "Exactly What It Shows," one design rule governs everything and one feeling governs everything.

**The rule (Devon's binding constraint, from Marcus Springboard F):** no client number appears in any asset without its conditions in the same visual unit. The condition sits at equal weight to the number. Never smaller, never gray, never a footnote.

**The feeling (Sarah's register, from Springboard C): the caveat is the credential.** The limit is not an apology bolted onto the claim. It is the thing that makes the claim believable. A confident practitioner stating a limit sounds like authority. An apologetic one sounds like they have nothing. We are the former, always.

**Strategic territory:** disciplined claiming as the thing InSync is known for. **Emotional territory:** the specific, underserved anxiety Marcus named — *the fear of being caught overclaiming.* This reader has stood in front of an executive committee, attributed a business result to a training program, and privately known that three other things also happened that quarter. They were not lying. They were compressing. They have carried a low-grade dread of the follow-up question ever since. The creative names that dread and resolves it: you are entitled to say more than you think, and less than you have been saying, and the more careful version is the more fundable version.

**What the creative must make the audience feel and recognize:** *The system pushed you toward the naked number. It was never you. Here is what it looks like to claim exactly what your data shows — the number and its conditions, standing together — and here is why that is the version an executive funds again.*

**What this week is NOT doing:**
- NOT re-opening the measurement gap. The reader closed that loop in July and Week 1. Do not restate 94 versus 13, do not re-explain that satisfaction is not impact, do not build any hero asset on the gap. The Dye bank may appear this week as **one line of established context only**, never as a lead or a hero. Lead nothing on 94 vs 13.
- NOT dumping proof. This is a credibility *escalation* week, not a proof-dump. We are not showing more numbers. We are showing better-formed ones.
- NOT running the naked outcome claim. A stat card that reads "35% engagement lift" with a logo and nothing else is the single most damaging asset we could make this week, because the reader who then opens Blog 2 meets an argument that indicts the very asset that brought them there. That is not a tone slip. It is a credibility contradiction that forfeits the week's entire position in one scroll. See Sections 5 and 7.
- NOT honesty performed as humility. "We don't have all the answers" is not this week. The register is precision, not modesty.

## 3. Visual Direction (for Devon)

Devon — you are extending the locked "On the Record" system, not opening a new one. Everything in your Week 1 design system (`03-devon-design-system.md`) still stands: the navy ground, the rationed teal, the muted-gray foil, off-white as material, the source stamp, the three faces, thumbnail-first, one idea per asset. Do not re-derive any of it. This section adds exactly one structural element and evolves the meaning of two colors. That is the whole delta.

**Color application — carried forward, with two deliberate evolutions.**
Palette is locked and unchanged: Deep Navy `#032B43`, Teal / InSync Cool Blue `#00B9F5`, Muted Gray `#8A97A6`, White / Off-white `#FFFFFF` / `#F4F1EA`. No fifth hue. No Golden Honey in any capacity.
- **Navy** — unchanged. The ground of proof, the "on the record" surface. 80%+ of every surface.
- **Teal** — evolves from "the payoff figure" to **"the bond between a number and its condition."** In Week 1 teal marked the 13, the one thing that mattered. In Week 2 teal marks the *pairing* — it is the spine that binds a client number to its conditions, because this week the pairing is the thing that matters. Still rationed. Still never decoration. When teal appears, it now says "this number and this condition belong together."
- **Muted gray** — evolves into a working tool. In Week 1 gray was "the easy data everyone collects." In Week 2 gray carries **the unconditioned number — the naked claim, the Organization A version.** Gray is now the color of a number stated without its conditions. This gives you a precise, honest visual argument for the comparison card: Organization A's claim renders in gray (a number missing something), Organization B's claim renders in white with a teal-bound conditions treatment (a number that is complete). **Critical exception: a condition is never gray.** Gray is for the number that lacks conditions, never for the condition itself.
- **Off-white** — unchanged. A material, breathing room, exhibit paper. Never tinted toward yellow.

**The one new structural element — the conditions treatment (extends the source stamp).**
Week 1's source stamp answered *who says so*: a thin teal rule plus a small mono citation line, bound to the number as one object. Week 2 adds a second element answering *what does this actually prove.* Same visual family. Same logic of number-and-attribution-as-one-object. Here is the mechanism I want you to design:

Beneath a client number, the stack now reads, top to bottom:
1. **The number** (Face 1, hero scale). Unchanged.
2. **The source stamp** (Week 1 element, unchanged): thin teal rule, mono/small-caps citation, quiet register. Answers *who*.
3. **The conditions bracket (NEW):** a two-line pair at **reading weight**, bound to the number by a short teal spine or bracket. Line one is what the number measured (the CAN). Line two is what it does not prove (the CANNOT). This maps directly onto Blog 2's "what data can and cannot show" frame — the framework anchor becomes the design mechanism.

Non-negotiable construction rules for the conditions bracket:
- **Equal weight.** The conditions bracket is set in Face 1 at or near the micro-label size (target 20–24 px at hero, never below the source-stamp size). It is **not** in the mono/citation register — the mono register reads as "quiet footnote," and the whole point is that the caveat is not a footnote. White or off-white type, never gray.
- **The CANNOT line is never subordinate to the CAN line.** Same size, same weight, same color. If anything, the CANNOT line is the one an informed reader respects most, so do not let it shrink.
- **Bound, not floated.** A teal spine, bracket, or tick physically ties the bracket to the number so they read as one unit at a glance. If the reader can mentally separate the number from its conditions, the treatment has failed.
- **Two-second legibility.** Marcus's competitive risk #3 is that a client-results asset looks like every other client-results asset in a feed. The conditions treatment is the only thing that makes ours visually distinct. If "number plus conditions" does not read in under two seconds at thumbnail, the differentiation is invisible and the asset is indistinguishable from a competitor's case-study card. Squint-test every stat asset at 150 px and confirm the conditions bracket survives.

Worked example (use exactly this language, per Section 7):
> **30%**
> `— InSync client program, healthcare technology` *(source stamp, mono, teal rule)*
> **measured:** increase in confidence and proficiency *(conditions bracket, white, reading weight, teal spine)*
> **does not prove:** it will repeat at the same rate in a different organization

**Typography.** Unchanged from Week 1. Face 1 (Inter/Archivo, tabular figures) for numerals and data; Face 2 (Source Serif/Newsreader) for interpretive one-liners; Face 3 (IBM Plex Mono / Space Mono or small-caps) for the source stamp only. The conditions bracket uses **Face 1 Medium** at reading size — deliberately *not* Face 3, because the citation register would make the caveat read as fine print. Sentence case throughout. Tabular figures where numbers stack or compare.

**Imagery.** Same discipline as Week 1: abstract data and evidence, artifact-of-record, honest charts only, no stock, no fake product dashboards, no red/alarm treatment. **The one change: human subjects now exist, but only inside the Leadership Video and assets harvested directly from it** (quote cards, video stills, the Tuesday clip). The stat assets, the comparison card, the newsletter and email exhibits remain human-free — evidence, not faces. Do not composite Karen or Jennifer into a stat card; their presence is the video's, and the video's authority is that it is real people talking, not a designed portrait. When you pull a still or quote card from the video, treat it as documentary (real frame, real caption) rather than a styled headshot.

**Iconography — the conditions-stamp extension.** The source stamp remains the core motif. The conditions bracket is its sibling, not a second motif competing for attention — same teal, same "bound to the number" logic, same quiet confidence. Think of them as two lines of the same citation: the source line (who) and the conditions line (what it proves). Together they are the visual embodiment of "exactly what it shows." Do not invent a third mark, a badge, a seal, or a checkmark/x-mark iconography for CAN/CANNOT — that reads as a pass/fail chart and cheapens the caveat into a warning. The CANNOT line is a credential, not an error state.

**Layout principles.** Unchanged: the number gets the room; the source gets its fixed place; teal gets the CTA and now the number-condition bond; white space is a feature; one idea per asset; thumbnail-first. New addition: **the conditions bracket gets reserved vertical space in every stat layout from the first sketch.** Do not design a number and then discover you have nowhere to put its conditions. The bracket is part of the number's footprint, always.

**The video lower-third is a real design problem — solve it deliberately.** LinkedIn video is watched silent, so every on-screen number in the Leadership Video needs its conditions visible, not merely spoken. Design a **motion version of the conditions treatment** for the lower third: when a speaker says "30%," the number appears with its source and its conditions bracket in the lower third, at equal weight, teal-bound, on screen long enough to read (target 4–5 seconds minimum for a two-line condition). This is the conditions stamp in motion and it is the hardest single design task of the week. Spec it as a repeatable lower-third template so every number in the video gets the same treatment and the 30-second script can reuse it.

**What to avoid, and why.**
- **Any client number without its conditions bracket** → the exact behavior the week argues against; the single most damaging self-inflicted wound available to us.
- **A condition rendered in gray, small, or as a footnote** → says "disclaimer," reads as weakness, forfeits the "caveat is the credential" position. Gray is for the unconditioned *number*, never for the *condition*.
- **CAN/CANNOT as checkmark/x iconography** → turns a credential into a pass/fail warning; also drifts toward alarm treatment, which is banned.
- **Lifting Blog 2's Organization A/B numbers (15%, 70%, 65%, 14%) into any card** → they are hypothetical, and in a week that also publishes real client numbers they will be read as client results. Relabel, genericize, or drop. See Section 7.
- **Re-running the 94 vs 13 hero card as a hero** → that was Week 1's flagship; here the gap is one line of context, never a hero object.
- **Golden Honey, a fourth accent, fake dashboards, generic stock, red/alarm** → all still banned, unchanged from Week 1.
- **Rendering "confidence and proficiency" as "performance" or "ROI" in any label** → this is the self-report exposure (Section 7); state exactly what was measured or we are exposed.

## 4. Tone of Voice (for Sarah)

**Governing principle, one sentence:** State something firm, then state its limit, in the same breath and in that order — because a number, its condition, and its limit standing together is the only claim this week will let you make.

The limit never comes first. "It's hard to prove any of this" is defeat. "We can tell you what changed, and we will not tell you it will change the same way somewhere else" is authority. Same fact, opposite positioning. Every client number you write carries its condition inline, the way every stat carried its source last week. Last week's rule was *every stat carries its source*. This week keeps that and adds: **every client number carries its conditions.**

**What it sounds like.**

*Newsletter (widest reach, leadership-first):*
> There is the number that impresses a room, and there is the number that survives the follow-up question. They are rarely the same number. In a healthcare technology program, confidence and proficiency rose 30%. That is what we measured, and that is what we will claim. We will not tell you it will rise 30% in your organization, because your conditions are not that organization's conditions. Here is how to know whether the conditions behind a result like that will hold in your own programs.

*Email #2 (highest-intent, second letter from the same person):*
> Last time, I showed you the research. This time, one question. When you last told your executive committee that a program worked, and someone asked how you knew, did your answer hold? Most of us have been in that room. The honest version of the story is the one that survives the question, and it is also the one that gets funded. The Live Learning Impact Diagnostic shows you where your live learning is reliable and where it depends on heroics, before you scale.

*LinkedIn, Wednesday (the contrast, most save-worthy):*
> Two teams. Same program. Same results. Organization A said: training drove a productivity increase. Organization B said what it observed, how many people applied it, over what window, and what else was going on that quarter. Which one would you fund next year? The honest version wins. Not because it is humble. Because a senior leader can tell the difference between a claim and an argument. (Illustration, not a client case.)

*Leadership Video voiceover / pull-quote register (Karen or Jennifer, named):*
> "We saw a 35% engagement lift in a consulting firm program. I can tell you the conditions that produced it. I can't tell you it will look identical in a different firm, and I wouldn't want a client to plan as if it will."

**What to avoid — wrong vs. right.**
- **The unqualified outcome claim.** Wrong: "35% engagement lift" standing alone, or "proven results," "guaranteed impact," "delivers measurable ROI." Right: the number, what it measured, and what it does not prove, together. These superlatives are the exact constructions Blog 2 dismantles; using them is self-refuting.
- **The limit first.** Wrong: "No data is perfect, but we saw a 30% lift." Right: "Confidence and proficiency rose 30%. Here is the condition, and here is what it does not prove." Firm first, always.
- **Honesty as humility.** Wrong: "We don't claim to have all the answers." Right: "We claim exactly what we measured." Precision, not modesty.
- **Inflating what was measured.** Wrong: "a 30% performance gain," "a 30% ROI." Right: "a 30% increase in confidence and proficiency." Say precisely what was measured. This is a protection, not a limitation — see Section 7.
- **Re-teaching the gap.** Wrong: "As the research showed, 94% measure satisfaction while only 13%..." as a lead. Right: the Dye figures appear at most as one line of established context, never a lead or hero. Do not lead anything on 94 vs 13 this week.
- **Either 73%, in short-form.** Do not use the Dye 73% or Blog 2's Kirkpatrick 73% in any short-form asset. Two different 73%s from two different sources are in play; the risk of a reader meeting the *other* 73% at the link is not worth the value. See Section 7.
- **Misattributing Blog 2's external stats.** Brandon Hall 55%, Saks & Belcort 30–40%, ATD $1,254 / 13.7 hours each belong to their own source, never to the InSync survey. Of these, Saks & Belcort (transfer decays without reinforcement) is the most useful for short-form because it supports the "conditions matter" argument directly. Do not use Blog 2's "60% decline since 2020" line at all.
- **Blog 2's Organization A/B numbers as real.** The 15% / 70% / 65% / 14% are hypothetical ("imagine two organizations"). Use the A/B structure as a device; relabel or drop the numbers. Never render them as client results.
- **Banned words, absolute:** best-in-class, synergy, leverage (as a verb), value-add, holistic, innovative (without specifics), transform, world-class. Also banned: the word **"campaign"** in reader-facing copy (use "this month," "September," "this conversation").
- **Mechanics, non-negotiable:** no em dashes; sentence case throughout including subject lines and headlines; every stat sourced inline and every client number conditioned inline; InSync is a **partner, never a vendor**; **no named webinar host** (use "our team" or "an InSync facilitator" — Jennifer bylines and speaks on the video, but is not confirmed as the webinar host); the primary CTA is the **Live Learning Impact Diagnostic** at `https://info.insynctraining.com/live-learning-impact-diagnostic`, described accurately per `00-diagnostic-brief-for-sarah.md`; the name **"Readiness Strategy Tool" is retired and appears nowhere.**

## 7. Audience Insight — The North Star

**The insight:** This reader's dominant emotion this week is not exposure (that was Week 1). It is **the fear of being caught overclaiming.** They have stood in a boardroom, attributed a result to a program, and privately known the attribution was a compression of a messier truth. They were not lying. The incentive structure pushed them toward the clean number. And they have carried a quiet dread of the one follow-up question they cannot answer ever since. What they lack is not honesty. It is *a way to be honest that still sounds authoritative* — a sentence structure that lets them state a limit without losing the room.

**The creative opportunity:** Model the exact sentence, the exact card, the exact on-screen moment where a confident person states a number, its condition, and its limit, and sounds *more* credible for it, not less. Hand the reader language and a template they can borrow for their own next executive update. When Wednesday's contrast card gets saved, it is because a leader intends to reuse the framing — that save is the purest evidence we handed someone words they mean to carry. Blame the incentive, never the reader: the framing is always *the system pushed you toward compression*, never *you have been overclaiming.*

**Prioritization:** When a decision must serve L&D Leadership or Practitioners at the other's expense, **serve Leadership.** The Diagnostic converts leaders; the Leadership Video is a leadership-register asset by construction; the trust we build leads to a consulting conversation, and practitioners do not buy consulting. Practitioners are served through Blog 2 (genuinely useful to them on its own), through the CAN/CANNOT list surfacing in the LinkedIn set, and by being handed forward to the webinar. **One deliberate exception:** the LinkedIn set is the one place practitioners are actively served, because they are the amplification layer and LinkedIn is where amplification happens.
