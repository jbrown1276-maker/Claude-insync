## 2. Target Audience Analysis

**This week flips the month's priority, and I am stating that explicitly.** Weeks 1 and 2 prioritized L&D Leadership: the Diagnostic converts leaders, the Leadership Video was a leadership-register asset, and practitioners were the amplification layer. **Week 3 inverts that. The primary activation target this week is Practitioners.** This is their week by design, and the justification is structural, not sentimental: the observation protocol is a practitioner practice, Blog 3 is written in a practitioner voice by a practitioner (Jen Finan), and the primary CTA is a practitioner guide for building a facilitator workflow. A leadership-first framing would break the week. You cannot hand a facilitator a real-time craft and narrate it to their boss.

**Primary activation target: Practitioners.** Facilitators, instructional designers, producers, and training managers. Every primary CTA, the carousel, Blog 3, the newsletter lead, and Email #3 are built for this person.

Psychographically, meet this person at a precise moment. They already know their sessions produce signal. They watch learners lean in and check out; they hear the clarifying question that means someone is testing the framework against their own situation; they feel the room go with them or against them. And they have watched that knowledge evaporate at the end of every session because nobody gave them a way to capture it. Their dominant emotion this week is not exposure or fear of being caught overclaiming (those were the leadership emotions of Weeks 1 and 2). It is **quiet professional frustration that their best evidence keeps disappearing** — combined with a well-earned defensiveness about anything that would split their attention while they teach. Blog 3 names this exactly: the data "lives in their head. It goes nowhere." The relief the week offers is that the fix is not a form during delivery. It is three to five minutes after, and a trained eye.

What resonates with them: the reframe that they are already observing and only need to systematize it; a concrete, named list of what to watch for so "notice more" becomes actionable; the explicit promise that the protocol does not steal focus during teaching ("you separate the moments"); the validation that the best facilitators already do this in their heads and the protocol just makes it repeatable across a team. The Saks & Belcort 30–40% finding lands here not as an indictment but as vindication: application decays without reinforcement, which is how learning works, not a facilitator's personal failure.

What does not resonate: boardroom framing with no Monday-morning takeaway; being told to add a task during delivery (the fastest way to lose this audience is to make the protocol sound like more work while teaching); academic language about "behavioral evaluation constructs"; and being talked *about* rather than *to*. This audience can smell leadership-register copy that was retrofitted for them, and it reads as condescension.

**Secondary amplification target: L&D Leadership.** CLO, VP of L&D, Director of Learning. Their role this week is different and worth serving precisely: **they are the ones who fund the practice.** A leader does not run an observation protocol, but a leader decides whether facilitators get trained on one and whether the organization treats facilitator observation as a legitimate evaluation source. Their version of this week's payoff is the "from individual insight to program intelligence" argument: observation data from every session compounds into design intelligence by session five, without waiting six months for survey results. That is a leadership-grade outcome built on a practitioner practice, and it is how the week hands back up to the funders without diluting the practitioner message.

**Explicit prioritization.** When a message, CTA, or design choice must serve one audience at the expense of the other, **serve Practitioners.** This is the inversion of Weeks 1 and 2 and it is intentional. The practitioner is the actor this week; the leader is the sponsor. Serve leadership through the newsletter's and the carousel's framing that this practice produces program-level intelligence, and through the webinar (which a leader may attend or send their team to). Do not dilute the practitioner craft message to add a boardroom layer. One deliberate exception: the newsletter may carry a single leadership-facing line about program intelligence, because the newsletter is the widest-reach owned channel and the most likely to reach a funder. Everywhere else, stay on the floor of the session.

## 3. Messaging Pillars and Hierarchy

**The One Thing.** The best evaluation data in your program is already in the room, in the facilitator's eyes, and a simple observation protocol turns what they already see into evidence that predicts transfer better than any post-course survey.

Everything downstream is a variation on that sentence. The word doing the work this week is *already*. Week 1's word was *proves*; Week 2's was *exact*; Week 3's is *already*. The practitioner does not need a new skill. They need a way to keep what they already do.

**Tier 1: Your facilitators are your best evaluation source, and most organizations never tap them.** *Strategic function: the reframe and the hook.* This is the opening beat, the newsletter lead, and the carousel's first card. The data with the highest predictive value sits with the person running the session, and then, in most organizations, it disappears. Stating that loss plainly is what earns the practitioner's attention, because it names a waste they have felt for years.

**Tier 2: Five behaviors tell you learning is happening, and a trained eye catches them in real time.** *Strategic function: the method, and the carousel's spine.* This is the heart of the week and the observation protocol itself. The five behaviors, in order: (1) a learner applies the framework to their own situation, unprompted; (2) a learner asks a clarifying question about a specific scenario; (3) learners name the gap between their current approach and the framework; (4) a learner pushes back or challenges the framework, which signals critical thinking, not resistance; (5) learners teach each other, which is peer learning. This tier is the portable, save-worthy artifact of the week. It is what a facilitator screenshots and brings to their next session.

**Tier 3: You do not add a task while teaching. You separate the moments.** *Strategic function: the objection-killer.* This is the tier that protects the whole argument, because the first thing every facilitator thinks is "I can't take notes while I teach." The answer is that you do not. During the session you facilitate. During a transition, at lunch, or at the end of the day, you spend three to five minutes documenting patterns you already noticed. If a producer supports the session, they can capture in real time. This tier converts the protocol from "one more thing" into "a way to keep what you were already going to lose," and it is the difference between the audience adopting the practice and dismissing it.

**Tier 4: Start building your protocol with the Applying the Research guide, and bring your questions to the September 24 webinar.** *Strategic function: the CTA logic and conversion bridge.* The Companion Guide is positioned as the practical walkthrough: what a facilitator observation protocol looks like, how to build it, how to train facilitators on it, how to turn the data into program improvements. The webinar is the secondary CTA, promoted through the carousel, with no named host. Tier 4 is the smallest tier by word count and the most important by function. Everything above it exists to make the guide a reasonable next click for someone who just recognized they are sitting on evidence.

**The message to AVOID this week, and what it would cost.**

Avoid **re-opening the measurement gap or re-arguing Week 2.** Do not restate that satisfaction is not impact, do not rebuild anything on the 94-versus-13 contrast or the two 73%s, and do not re-run the "number plus conditions" argument as though it were new. The reader closed those loops in Weeks 1 and 2. Repeating them makes Conviction Month sound like a loop instead of a build, and it wastes the one week the campaign has to move from principle to practice. The Dye research may appear this week only as a single line of established context, never as a lead or a hero number.

Avoid, more sharply, **framing the protocol as an added task during delivery.** The single costliest failure available this week is copy or a carousel card that makes observation sound like note-taking while teaching. It would trigger the exact objection that makes practitioners abandon evaluation tools, and it would contradict Tier 3 in the same breath as stating it. If any asset shows a facilitator distracted by a form mid-session, we have argued against ourselves.

Third, avoid **leadership register.** "Drive measurable evaluation outcomes across your L&D function" is correct for Weeks 1 and 2 and wrong for Week 3. It talks over the practitioner's head to a funder who is not the primary audience this week. If the copy sounds like a boardroom, the practitioner stops reading, and we forfeit the one week built specifically for them.

## 5. Tone and Positioning Guardrails

**What it must sound like.** Practical, direct, and collegial. The register of an experienced facilitator talking to another facilitator about something that actually works in the room. This is the Jen Finan practitioner voice, and it is the calibration model for the whole week (with Blog 1 and Blog 4 as the reference registers per the voice-tone guide, not the leadership register of Weeks 1 and 2). Short declarative sentences. Second person. Concrete over abstract. The reader should feel recognized, not instructed.

Concrete examples of the right register:

- "You're already watching this. You just haven't been writing it down."
- "You don't take notes while you teach. You take three to five minutes after, when your brain isn't split."
- "When a manager pushes back on the framework, that's not resistance. That's someone thinking."
- "The best facilitators already do this in their heads. A protocol just makes it repeatable across your team."

Note what these share: each speaks to the practitioner as a peer who already has the instinct, and offers structure rather than correction. The voice validates first, then systematizes.

**What to avoid, specifically.**

Avoid **leadership abstraction:** "operationalize behavioral evaluation," "drive evaluation maturity across the function," "align measurement to business outcomes." Correct for Weeks 1 and 2, wrong here. It talks over the practitioner to a funder.

Avoid **making the protocol sound like work during delivery:** any construction that implies note-taking, form-filling, or divided attention while teaching. "Track each learner on a scoring sheet as you go" is the anti-message. Compare it to "notice the behavior, note the pattern after" — same practice, opposite feeling.

Avoid **turning the illustrative example into data.** Blog 3's "8 of 12 managers, 3 clarifying questions, 2 pushed back" is a teaching illustration inside a hypothetical difficult-conversations program. It is not an InSync result and not a benchmark. If it appears anywhere, it is labeled as an example.

Enforce the InSync banned list absolutely: **best-in-class, synergy, leverage as a verb, value-add, holistic, innovative without specifics, transform, world-class.** Also banned: the word "campaign" in any reader-facing copy. InSync is a **partner**, never a **vendor.**

Mechanical rules, carried forward and non-negotiable: **no em dashes** (prefer short declarative sentences), sentence case throughout, every statistic carries its source inline, and no named webinar host.

**Attribution guardrail, scoped for Week 3.** Every stat carries its source. This week has two external stats and one illustrative example, so the rule is specific: **Saks & Belcort 30–40%** attributes to Saks & Belcort, never to InSync; **Brandon Hall Group 2023 L&D Benchmarking Survey 77%** (collaborative learning; top-six modality) attributes to Brandon Hall, never to InSync; and the **8-of-12 example** is labeled as an illustration, never as a result. There are no InSync client numbers this week, so the conditions bracket is not load-bearing here. The discipline that carries forward is the practice it stands for — observe, document, iterate — which is now the subject of the content itself rather than a design constraint.

## 6. Competitive Landscape Summary

**Who else makes this argument.** The "prove learning's ROI" conversation is crowded, and the "here is what your proof doesn't prove" conversation (Week 2's territory) is thin. Week 3's territory — **the facilitator as a primary, systematic evaluation-data source** — is thinner still, and that is the opportunity. Most of the market treats evaluation as something that happens *after* the session, through surveys and dashboards. The idea that the richest, most predictive data is generated *during* delivery and captured by the facilitator is genuinely underclaimed.

Where adjacent voices exist, they come at it from different doors. Robert Brinkerhoff's Success Case Method looks for evidence in what learners actually did, but it works retrospectively through interviews, not real-time observation. Will Thalheimer's LTEM pushes evaluation toward behavior and transfer, philosophically aligned, but it is a measurement framework, not a facilitator craft. Roy Pollock and the 6Ds school emphasizes transfer and manager reinforcement, adjacent to the Saks & Belcort point, but again from the design and follow-up side, not the live-session-observation side. Producer-supported delivery and real-time behavioral observation is a space where InSync has standing that pure-methodology voices do not.

**InSync's differentiated angle.** InSync's entire model is built on **live, virtual, producer-supported delivery.** That is the defensible reason the observation protocol is not just a nice idea but something InSync can credibly teach: in an InSync session there is often a producer who can capture observations in real time while the facilitator teaches, which resolves the exact objection (Tier 3) that would sink the practice for a solo facilitator. An async-first platform cannot observe whether a learner asked an application question, and a self-paced course has no facilitator to watch for the five behaviors at all. InSync's format makes real-time behavioral observation *possible*, and the producer role makes it *practical*. That is a costly-to-copy advantage, rooted in how InSync actually runs sessions.

**Competitive risks.**

*One: the practice reads as obvious, so the reader does not act.* "Facilitators should pay attention to learners" can sound like something everyone already knows, which risks a nod without a click. Mitigation is Tier 2's specificity: the five named behaviors and the three-to-five-minute method turn a platitude into a protocol. The differentiation is in the systematization, and the copy must make the systematization the point, not the observation.

*Two: the objection kills adoption before the guide does.* If the "how do I do this while teaching" objection is not answered fast and credibly, the practitioner dismisses the whole thing as impractical and never reaches the CTA. Tier 3 is the mitigation and it must appear early, not as a footnote.

*Three: category sameness in the feed.* A LinkedIn carousel about "5 things to watch for" looks like a thousand generic tips carousels at a glance. The InSync distinctions (real-time, producer-supported, predicts transfer better than surveys) are what separate it, and Devon's treatment has to make those distinctions legible fast. The visual system (navy `#032B43`, teal `#00B9F5`, muted gray `#8A97A6`) is the recognizability anchor and must be extended, not rebuilt.
