Title:	Evaluation of Learning & Development - Industry Practices & Recommendations

Subtitle: Author Foreword

While attending a multi-week organizational behavior and dynamics program in New York, an old friend of mine remarked that Acme Rocket Corp (name redacted for obvious reasons) doesn't hold training because they're an altruistic organization. They hold it because they want to make the best rockets on the planet, faster than everyone else, and make more money than anyone else doing it.

That conversation stayed with me because it articulates what every organization claims to believe: training exists to address business needs. This isn't a new insight. Kirkpatrick told us in 1959 that evaluation should measure organizational impact. Phillips built ROI models around it. The principle has been settled for six decades. Training serves business objectives, not learner satisfaction.

The gap between what organizations claim to want of training and what they actually measure is the real story. This research documents that gap at scale. The idea that training should align with strategy isn't novel. What is novel - and what this data reveals - is the degree to which organizations fail to execute it. When 83 percent of evaluation practitioners feel their data doesn't answer the relevant business questions, and 73 percent of cannot articulate how their findings influence management decisions, we are not discussing edge cases. We are discussing an industry-wide problem.

I have worked in program evaluation for many years in various roles: naval officer, certification authority, program administrator, etc. After reviewing this data extensively, I was disheartened. To see that the vast majority of organizations of all sizes are failing to identify and measure organizational outcomes resulting from training implementation strikes me as an extraordinarily expensive way to operate. We are throwing expensive darts with a blindfold on with no way to tell whether we hit the board or not. Instead, evaluations rely on measure relating to learner feedback and delivery mechanics (in part, at least, because that data is easy to collect).

This is not to say that learner feedback isn't important. An engaged learner is far more likely to perform better, apply their learning, and retain knowledge. The problem is that many organizations stop there. Of the organizations using Kirkpatrick and Phillips ROI frameworks in this study, about one in eight collected data to look beyond individual outcomes to organizational impact. Yet organizational outcomes are the entire reason the training was delivered in the first place. I cannot understand how anyone can claim return on investment when they have not measured what was returned.

What I care about, as a business leader, is whether productivity goes up, whether error rates go down, whether the organization grows. That is what Acme cared about. That is why we were there - Acme had determined it needed our management team to attend the training to grow as an organization. My friend was right about that fundamental truth. And this research shows that truth is being ignored across the industry.

Title:	Evaluation of Learning & Development - Industry Practices & Recommendations

Subtitle: Executive Summary

Text:

One of the many recurring themes of trade literature in Learning and Development is Return on Investment (ROI). ROI is, at its essence, a measure of the effect of training on an organization and the costs of achieving that effect, yet a vast majority of practitioners in industry choose to focus on easily measured tangibles rather than actually answering the REAL question – “Did the training we conducted achieve what we wanted it to?”

In short, a discussion about ROI is really a discussion about cost and consequence – organizations leverage resources (people, time, money) and seek to achieve a particular outcome (compliance, competitive advantage, workforce development, etc.) with training. An investigation into ROI must therefore begin with the organizational objective rather than with data. Our survey of industry practices suggests that instead, a majority of L&D practitioners focus on the data collected (and in particular, easily collected data about learner affective response) rather than answer the question at hand.

The consequence of focusing on the wrong question cannot be overstated. Aggregated learner affective response data can be plugged into a evaluation model and suggest positive outcomes of the training implementation, when in fact, the connection between the training implementation and the organizational objective has never been explicitly established which in turn leads to improper conclusions about the training implementation.

In the present study, respondents were asked about their impressions of the evaluation of training approach used in their organization. For many respondents in prior studies, the process of evaluation was largely outside of their experience – data was provided in and feedback received – this was particularly true for those in a direct-learner interaction role (instructor, coach, etc.) (N=600). In contrast, the population sampled in this study - those tasked with evaluation of training (N=200) – a vast majority (83%) felt constrained by the data model in use and that the data provided an inaccurate or incomplete assessment of the training implementation. To wit – the wrong question was being answered.

This research effort was undertaken to explore the current training evaluation practices in industry and identify both best practices and recommendations of the evaluation of training.  In the present study, InSync conducted an exploratory sequential mixed methods study consisting of semi-structured interviews with industry practitioners (N-24) and directed sampling of the populations of interest (N=200)  that evaluated training or reviewed and interpreted evaluation data relating to training at least quarterly across all industry sectors. The instrument was designed in three parts - the first part of the instrument was designed to collect respondent observations and impressions of their instructional experience, the second part was designed to assess the individual practitioners response to positive statements about the evaluation of training within their organization, and the third part was designed to collect qualitative data about the impressions of the practitioners on the evaluation practices in their organization. 

The following are the principal conclusions and recommendations relating to the evaluation of training in the industry:

Most organizations evaluate some training: Data reflects that nearly all  organizations (most of whom reported themselves as “heavily regulated”) were evaluating at least some of their training deliveries, with a weighted mean of 51.7% of all training deliveries.

The evaluation of different types of training is…different: 96% of practitioners indicated that Structured Training delivered within their respective organizations was formally evaluated using an established process and/or model. That statistic drops to 79% for Ad-Hoc Training, and 29% for On-the-Job Training (OJT).

A vast majority (83%) of respondents felt constrained by the evaluation data model in use and that the data itself provided an inaccurate or incomplete assessment of the training implementation. Respondent qualitative data revealed two main issues: The evaluation did not provide a meaningful detail of the training and its outcomes (good or bad) or that the evaluation data provided no insight regarding the underlying organizational goals of the training. To wit – the respondents felt that the wrong question(s) was being answered.

Evaluation Approach: A modest majority of respondents (54.5%) reported that their organization utilized Key Performance Indicators (KPI’s) (leveraging Kirkpatrick or Phillips ROI as a framework) as their evaluation approach. Data relating to completion rates, affective response, resource costs, and assessment data were commonly cited as data sources. 

What is being measured:  A minority (13%) of respondents reported data sources in their evaluation model that are related or relevant to business outcomes (this includes inferred business relevant data sources when training was delivered to address a specific business need – e.g. safety stand-down, etc.).

Among respondents, 24% suggested that more comprehensive data relating to learner engagement, task performance/productivity, or behavioral change were integrated into their evaluation model. Moving beyond the training itself, only 14% of respondents used data relating to long-term learner outcomes, engagement, or follow-on career/vocational trajectory in evaluating the instructional program.

Few respondents (24%) could identify data collected in their evaluation approach that was directly related to an explicit specific metric related to the organizational goal of an evaluated instructional intervention. This is not to say that the training was not related to the organization’s performance, but that no data was being reported about how that training program did (or did not) “move the needle” on the organizational goal.

Within respondent data, the most common data type collected in training program evaluation was affective student response (94%), followed closely by delivery mechanics (duration, attendance, etc.). Treatment and inclusion of assessment data was mixed – most training delivered by respondent organizations were not assessed.  Qualitative data collected was rarely used by respondents in any systematic evaluation sense (3.5%). From interview data, direct quotations and similar anecdotal usage was the most common (75%) use of qualitative data.

Of programs where learner assessment was completed (48%), most assessments (86%) were completed by the organization, with the balance conducted by a third-party testing/certification authority or by a professional certification examination (trades licensing tests, professional certification examination, etc.).  In those cases, assessment data was not consistently included as part of the training program evaluation data model, with only 53% including that data in their evaluation approach of the training program.

A large majority of respondents (73%) could not articulate specific uses of the evaluation data by management in the execution of workforce training.  The primary (81%) perceived use was that of resource justification for sustaining or terminating a particular program.  Other uses included initiation of supplemental training, changing format of training delivery, and delivery of training to additional audiences within the organization.

The respondent qualitative data provide some clear insights into effective evaluation practices, but it is also apparent that a large majority of instructional practice in the industry reflects an unsupported use of marginally relevant data in lieu of other opportunities to meaningfully assess the effects of training on the organization.  Moreover, a significant population of practitioners felt that the evaluation model and data used provided an incomplete picture of training implementations in their organization, and few identified any relationship between organizational objective and the training evaluation process and reported evaluation results.  In this report, we will further expand on these areas for learning and development stakeholders so that they can implement a more effective training evaluation strategy that is directly tied to organizational objectives and enhances instructional outcomes for all learners.

Title:  Research of Industry Practices in  the Evaluation of Training

Subtitle: What is the State of Practice, and How Might It Be Improved

Reason for the Research:

One of the many recurring themes of trade literature in Learning and Development is Return on Investment (ROI). ROI is, at its essence, a measure of the effect of training on an organization and the costs of achieving that effect, yet a vast majority of practitioners in industry choose to focus on easily measured tangibles rather than actually answering the REAL question – “Was the training we conducted achieve what we wanted it to?”

In short, a discussion about ROI is really a discussion about cost and consequence – organizations leverage resources (people, time, money) and seek to achieve a particular outcome (compliance, competitive advantage, workforce development, etc.) with training. An investigation into ROI must therefore begin with the organizational objective rather than with data. Our survey of industry practices suggests that instead, a majority of L&D practitioners focus on the data collected (and in particular, easily collected data about learner affective response) rather than answer the question at hand.

The consequence of focusing on the wrong question cannot be overstated. Aggregated learner data can be plugged into a Key Performance Indicator (KPI) Model and suggest positive outcomes of the training implementation, when in fact, the connection between the training implementation and the organizational objective has never been explicitly established which in turn leads to improper conclusions about the training implementation.

Summary of the Study: 

In the present study, respondents were asked about their impressions of the evaluation of training approach used in their organization. For many respondents in prior studies, the process of evaluation was largely outside of their experience – data was provided in and feedback received – this was particularly true for those in a direct-learner interaction role (instructor, coach, etc.) (N=600). In contrast, the population sampled in this study (N=200) was directly tasked with either conducting training evaluations or interpreting or acting on the results of the evaluation.

In the present study, InSync conducted an exploratory sequential mixed methods study consisting of directed sampling of the population of interest that evaluated training or reviewed and interpreted evaluation data relating to training at least quarterly across all industry sectors. A basic interpretive study using purposeful stratified sampling was conducted consisting of semi-structured interviews with industry practitioners (N=24) regarding their impressions of industry practice and the methodology within their organizations. Interview data were open coded to contextualize the data into categories (Pidgeon, 1996).  Axial coding was performed concurrently by the researcher and a peer researcher to facilitate the identification and refinement of categories into concepts and defining how these concepts are related (Ruona, 2005). Member checks were conducted with participants where supplemental data or clarification was desired and to ensure that the coding adhered to the initial intent of the participant (Merriam & Grenier, 2019; Shenton, 2004). The constant comparison method of analysis (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) was employed to identify the central concepts by persistent review of all data from all sources to establish and refine the relationship of the central concepts to other concepts that are identified.   At every stage, the researcher sought validation of qualitative data through triangulation method and reviews for internal consistency within item responses (Shenton, 2004).  The central concepts identified in this analysis, along with the results of a comprehensive literature review, were used as factors in subsequent instrument development (Bulger, Mayer, Almeroth, & Blau, 2008).

The instrument was designed in three parts - the first part of the instrument was designed to collect respondent data and organizational context, the second part was designed to assess the individual practitioner’s affective response to positive statements about the evaluation of training within their organization, and the third part was designed to collect qualitative data about the impressions of the practitioners on the evaluation practices in their organization. 

Descriptive statistical analysis and multivariate analysis of variance and inferential statistics (t-test) were used to assess what types of evaluation methodologies provided enhanced reported satisfaction with evaluation results from both instructional and organizational perspectives. 

Operational Definitions

This report will use a variety of learning and development technical terms, many of which are commonly understood.  However, some of the terms used in this report can have multiple meanings – to provide clarity and concise usage, the following operational definitions for this report are used:

Ad-hoc Training is an instructional intervention conducted to address a specific, (often time-critical) purpose on an as-needed basis rather than as part of a structured curriculum.

Decision-oriented Evaluation is designed to provide program managers and key stakeholders with actionable data to make informed choices rather than simply generating theoretical results. These approaches are meant to guide continuous program improvement, planning, implementation, and future funding.

On-the-job training (OJT) is contextualized learning method where individuals acquire and refine job-related concepts and skills directly in the workplace from peers interaction, social modeling, task support resources, and formative feedback (see, e.g., Social Cognitive Theory).

Program-oriented Evaluation is a systematic approach to assessing a specific project or policy's efficiency, effectiveness, and assessed worth. It is a practical method to determine if a structured instructional intervention is meeting its defined objectives and goals.

Structured Training is an instructional approach characterized by explicit, systematic, and cumulative conceptual and/or skill mastery. Structured training often is developed and delivered in dedicated sessions tied to specific milestones of development or achievement.

Research Questions

The four research questions addressed in this study were:

In what ways does the industry conduct program evaluation of learning and development efforts? (RQ1)
How are the results of learning and development program evaluation used in industry? (RQ2)
What are the current “best practices” of formal program evaluation as applied to learning and development programs (planning, implementation, evaluation)? (RQ3)
How can program evaluation of learning and development enhance achievement of organizational outcomes? (RQ4)

Regarding these questions, qualitative data analysis and content validation was conducted based on data drawn from interviews with training program evaluation stakeholders in the instructional services industry.

Title: Current Research in Training Evaluation

Subtitle: Literature Review and Current Research

Text:

Program Evaluation Methodologies

Training has become so commonplace in organizations that we forget to ask why they are being run in the first place. From an organizational perspective, training is meant to do concrete work, such as build capability, close performance gaps, or connect learning to strategic goals (Ruiz et al., 2020). This makes training less of a standalone event and more like a tool to help improve productivity, safety, customer outcomes, or whatever the organization needs to change. At the same time, the idea of “what training is for” looks different depending on perspective. Typically, learners will talk about personal growth and skill development, while organizations consider measurable outcomes, behavior change, and ROI (Neendoor, 2024; Kanaki, 2024). That split matters because it shapes what people expect evaluations to answer.

If an evaluation is going to be useful, it has to start with clear goals and objectives — what, exactly, the program is trying to accomplish — because those goals determine what evidence counts as success (McSweeney, 2024). When goals are vague or missing, evaluation tends to default to whatever is easy to collect rather than what is meaningful (LevinRozalis & Rosenstein, 2005; Ruiz et al., 2020). Over and over, research points to the same idea that you design differently when you know how you will measure success.

There is a small set of well-known models that shape how practitioners think about evaluation. Kirkpatrick’s Four Levels (Reaction, Learning, Behavior, Results) continues to appear in the literature because it provides a simple language to categorize outcomes (Cahapay, 2021). While its simplicity appears as one of the model’s strengths, it is important to note that after implementation, organizations often stop at reaction and learning measures and never push to behavior and results, which are the parts that determine business impact (Cahapay, 2021). Over time, other approaches have been established to broaden the scope. Phillips’ ROI model explicitly asks for financial translation of outcomes, CIPP (Context, Input, Process, Product) invites evaluators to look across the whole program lifecycle, and CIRO and Kaufman’s levels encourage thinking about needs and system outcomes beyond immediate learning (Del Birut, 2025; Neendoor, 2024; Stufflebeam, 1983). Methods such as Brinkerhoff’s Success Case Method and Provus’ Discrepancy Evaluation Model move the discussion from average effects to practical stories and gaps, looking at who succeeded and why, where objectives were missed, and what local conditions shaped those results (Deller, 2021; Downes, 2015; Provus, 1969).

Research conducted from more applied sources ties those models back to daily practice. For example, public health guidance recommends planning evaluation early and choosing methods that match the purpose. The CDC (2025) recommends following formative process checks while a course is being built and summative outcome checks afterward. Practitioner guides add a toolbox of commonly used techniques. Often this includes pre/post-tests, reaction surveys, interviews, focus groups, observations, performance metrics, completion data, and sometimes ROI calculations (Del Birut, 2025; Kanaki, 2024; Neendoor, 2024). Taken together, these tools make clear that no single method answers every question. 

Another repeated point in the literature is that evaluation should be embedded in design, not added afterwards (CDC, 2025). When evaluation is planned from the start, design choices, learning activities, and measures align; when it is an afterthought, organizations tend to collect what’s easy, which usually means reaction surveys that tell you little about transfer or impact.

Evaluation of Training: Current Research

Recent research stresses the same practical themes while also documenting gaps between ideals and practice. Scholars argue that evaluation is most valuable when it links directly to organizational goals and when it reaches beyond simple satisfaction or knowledge checks (Ruiz et al., 2020; McSweeney, 2024). Satisfaction scores are not useless as they give a quick pulse, but they don’t tell you whether learners apply new skills or whether the organization actually benefits (Townley, 2023; Kanaki, 2024). Because of that shortcoming, many researchers recommend mixed methods: combine pre/post assessments, behavioral observation, performance indicators, interviews, and followup measures weeks or months after training (CDC, 2025; Del Birut, 2025; Neendoor, 2024).

At the same time, empirical studies repeatedly find that organizations frequently fall short of these recommendations. Evaluation often remains limited to endofcourse questionnaires and brief knowledge checks because they are simple to administer and report (Ruiz et al., 2020; Townley, 2023). Resource limits, lack of expertise, and cultural resistance all show up as barriers since organizations may not have the time, data access, or analytic capability to pursue longerterm or behavior-focused measures, and sometimes there’s anxiety about what a rigorous evaluation might reveal (Ruiz et al., 2020; Townley, 2023). The combination of these restrictions helps explain why the practical picture looks different from the models.

Additionally, the literature emphasizes the social and organizational uses of evaluation. Evaluation yields the most value when results are communicated, discussed, and acted upon, often seen when findings become part of organizational learning rather than a static report (LevinRozalis & Rosenstein, 2005). That requires attention to utility and feasibility as evaluation standards. Evidence should be accurate and ethical, but it also needs to be timely and usable for stakeholders (Mertens et al., 2025). In other words, a technically “perfect” study that no one reads or uses has missed the point.

The current literature also nudges future evaluators to consider practical alignment and match methods to the training type and the intended outcomes. Compliance programs, where the immediate goal is demonstration of knowledge or completion, will rely on different indicators than leadership development or complex skill training, which require observation, reflective practice, and transfer measures (Kanaki, 2024; Neendoor, 2024). The research thus suggests a pragmatic orientation where those in the evaluation or design phases choose the measures that speak to the change you want to see.

Industry Practices in Learning Evaluation

When we look at industry practice, the picture is familiar since most organizations evaluate training in some form, but what they collect and how they use it varies widely (Ruiz et al., 2020; Townley, 2023). A common pattern is reliance on quick, administratively simple measures — reaction surveys, completion rates, and short knowledge checks — rather than sustained followup that would show transfer and impact (Cahapay, 2021; Townley, 2023). The pattern is understandable given the pressures L&D teams face, but it also means that many evaluations stop short of answering the question organizations often care about most: did performance change?

The research and industry literature suggest that the methods used often depend on the training’s purpose. Compliance and mandatory trainings tend to use completion and test scores because those indicators satisfy regulatory and accountability needs; skills or leadership programs more often require observation, manager feedback, or longitudinal measures if the organization pursues them (Kanaki, 2024; Neendoor, 2024). In practice, this means that different programs within the same company may be evaluated very differently and that the most important business questions sometimes remain unmeasured.

Current Industry Practices – Collection and Uses of Evaluation Data

In most workplaces, the data people collect are what they can collect, such as posttraining surveys, LMS completion reports, short quizzes, and usage metrics (CDC, 2025; Kanaki, 2024; Neendoor, 2024). When organizations have greater data capabilities, they add performance metrics, observational checklists, or manager assessments, and ROI or costbenefit calculations (Del Birut, 2025; Ruiz et al., 2020). Timing also varies. Immediate feedback is common and convenient, but it captures only a snapshot. Fewer organizations systematically follow up later to measure transfer or on-the-job behavior (Townley, 2023; Kanaki, 2024). When a follow-up does happen, it is often prompted by a strong business need (for example, when training is linked to a critical safety outcome or a costly compliance requirement) rather than a routine part of every program.

How organizations use data also depends on internal roles and communication. In some places, the designers collect and interpret evaluation data and then iterate on the program. In others the responsibility sits with HR, operations, or a separate analytics function; in many cases results are aggregated and reported upward for accountability without clear follow-through to operational change (LevinRozalis & Rosenstein, 2005; Del Birut, 2025). If evaluation is used well, it becomes part of continuous improvement: findings feed the curriculum changes, facilitation adjustments, additional coaching, or follow-up resources. If used poorly, evaluation feels like paperwork and becomes seen as a required formality that produces minimal tangible change (Ruiz et al., 2020).
Perceptions of Evaluation in Practice in Organizations

How people think about evaluation affects whether it will matter. Practitioners who see evaluation as a tool for learning and improvement are more likely to design useful measures and to champion applying results (Del Birut, 2025; Neendoor, 2024). Conversely, when evaluation is seen primarily as compliance or a checkbox, it is less likely to influence practice (Townley, 2023). Perception ties back to organizational culture and leadership. When leaders ask evaluation questions that connect to business goals and make time for discussing findings, the evaluation has a higher chance of informing decisions (LevinRozalis & Rosenstein, 2005).

Communication is crucial here. If evaluation results are siloed and reported only to one team or buried in dashboards, they rarely prompt change. When results are shared with designers, facilitators, managers and leaders, and when those groups collaborate on interpreting the data, evaluation becomes a driver of program improvement (LevinRozalis & Rosenstein, 2005; Del Birut, 2025). It requires not just data collection, but attention to how results are presented and to whom. They should be practical, timely summaries aimed at the right audience to make the difference.

Finally, industry literature emphasizes that certain constraints shape practice as much as theory. Time, budget, data access, and analytic skills all limit what organizations can realistically do (Ruiz et al., 2020). For L&D teams working within those restrictions, the best route is often to choose a small set of meaningful indicators, plan evaluation early, and build simple followup checks that can be executed reliably. We should aim for measures that are useful and feasible, while still treating evaluations as part of the design process, rather than as an afterthought after training has been completed.

Subtitle: It All Comes Back to Organizational Objectives

Text:

Decision Oriented Evaluation

In general terms, evaluation of training programs is necessary to gain insight into the program progress, functionality to the organization, and outcomes. A systematically structured program evaluation approach can assess whether the program is fulfilling required instructional needs while concurrently assessing whether the training is in alignment and advancing the goals and objectives of the organization. As noted in the literature review, the formal field of program management contains several classifications of approaches/frameworks, including:

Program Based Evaluation
Decision Based Evaluation
Consumer-Based Evaluation
Expertise Based Evaluation
Participant Based Evaluation

These approaches are applicable based on the purpose and desired use of evaluation data. Within most hierarchical organizations, training evaluation is appropriately decision based, as the goal is not to necessarily determine if training is required or use learner feedback as the ultimate measure of program efficacy (although many seem to), but rather how to improve training delivery and efficacy in achieving organizational goals. 

Evaluating instructional programs is a complex task. Data collection, analysis and reporting alone can be a challenge, but what emerges from the data is a picture wherein the evaluation almost seems pro-forma – “we’re evaluating the instruction because we’re supposed to evaluate the program.” To illustrate the state of practice, let’s examine using KPI’s to evaluate training. As noted in this report, using KPI’s to evaluate training is the most popular methodology identified by respondents. A casual review of industry literature suggests that using KPI’s  provides excellent insight into the execution of training in an organization, often within a framework such as Kirkpatrick to establish a connection between organizational goals and the training delivered. Indeed, quantitative data is easy to understand and extend into use cases – KPI’s in other aspects organizational performance are critical to advancing productivity and effectiveness. 

The shortcoming revealed by the data in this study suggests a sharp disconnect between learning outcomes and organizational outcomes. The collection, aggregation, and reporting of data lacks the transition from status reporting to one of decision-oriented evaluation. Put simply, the failure of actively linking data with the underlying organizational goals makes evaluation approaches as implemented in most organizations ineffective, leading 83% of respondent practitioners to suggest that their respective evaluation approaches are not telling the whole story. 

A Proposed Evaluation Approach

An effective methodology for programmatic training evaluation will support a sustainable solution meant to facilitate decision making, transparency, and effective communication within the organization. There are many evaluation approaches that can be effective (including a variety identified by respondents) that treat and report data in a  variety of methods. Within the data from this study, identified successful approaches do have some common elements. Those common elements include:

		Explicit statement of desired business outcomes from the training program;
		Development of a robust data model that supports measuring those desired business outcomes;
		Clear identification of data sources, collection periodicity, analysis approach, etc.;
		Identification of key stakeholders and information needs;
		Communication and reporting requirements to satisfy those needs; and
		Documentation and endorsement of the evaluation approach.

To illustrate an effective approach, InSync utilizes an expertise-modified CIPP (Stufflebeam, 1969) decision-oriented evaluation approach that is systematic in nature includes context elements and real-world limitations and follows a process of delineating, obtaining, reporting and applying information to make decisions. Perhaps most importantly, the organizational objectives and outcomes  (short-, mid-, and long-term) are explicitly articulated and anticipated in the evaluation and measurement plan when evaluating the training.  

The evaluation approach follows an approach focusing on the needs of the training program decision maker within the context of the training program and its internal and external influences, identifying what information is required for those decisions, defining that informational need into construct(s) for measurement, collecting, organizing, and analyzing the information, and reporting it out to the appropriate decision maker(s) in the program. Evaluation Questions & Constraints

Training program evaluation must always start with the questions to be answered, generally, evaluation questions should focus on the planning/implementation aspects of current and potential aspects of the training, as well as the broader questions related to the measurement, governance, communication strategies, and decision making within the program and organization. It is anticipated that during the evaluation process, additional questions may arise that require treatment and analysis as part of this evaluation.  The primary questions that are typically addressed in are as follows:

Question 1:	 What are the organizational objectives of the training program implementation?

Question 2:  Has the training program implementation improved organizational performance and instructional treatment through identified activities and measures?

Question 3:  Has learner performance in the targeted populations improved based on the training program implementation?

Question 4:  Has the broader stakeholder population derived operational benefits from the implementation of the training program implementation?

Question 5: What are “best practices” in the context of planning implementation, internal evaluation, performance monitoring, capacity building and sustainability of internal organizational learning?

To adequately treat the questions of this evaluation approach, the evaluator must identify key constraints on both the organization and the training program. These constraints, in turn, will inform data collection, stakeholder engagement, analysis, and reporting processes.  The key constraints will be managed as programmatic risks in the evaluation process and characterized as to the likelihood and severity of impact of the risk, as well as risk mitigation through engineered and process controls.

The inclusion of formal expertise in the evaluation model provides for opportunities to incorporate processes in all aspects of the training program, including planning, data collection, professional development of participating stakeholders, and reporting/monitoring.  In the context of planning, the effort directed at developing a data collection protocol and process relies on extensive review of program documentation and a variety of other data sources (site visits, program stakeholders, etc.) to ensure data is collected efficiently and with a minimum of resources.  The data collection plan is adapted a priori to the realities of the program on the ground and the exigencies of program execution, thereby providing relevance to both the primary intended user of the evaluation results (the program sponsor) and other stakeholders (participants, administrators, training staff, training sponsors, etc.).

Data Collection
			
The data collection and reporting under this evaluation approach provides transparency and actionable data to a variety of training stakeholder populations. The principal risk of this capability is one of validity of the measures – the use of data-based decision making is fraught with anecdotes of inappropriate interpretation of data in construct-irrelevant contexts (Straub and Welke, 1998).  

In this case, the measures and rubric to be applied are operationalized prior to collection – subsequent distribution to and relevancy for each stakeholder population will be derived by their need for the data – all stakeholders will have more information upon which to act and make decisions within their respective domains.  Moreover, informing all stakeholder groups about what is to be collected and how the data is to be used provides an opportunity to develop and foster communication with other stakeholders in the program, develop insight into their perspectives as they relate to program effectiveness under the measures developed, and enhance measure validity.  

Measurement, Governance and Communication Strategies 

With the development of a robust program of meaningful measures and reporting, program evaluation facilitates program transparency, governance, and effective planning.  Stakeholders across the spectrum of responsibility of the training function are able to internally assess and take action to modify the program as it operates.  The goal of the program evaluation step is to provide a means of low risk, high-reward sustainability of the program, defining success in ways that advance  organizational objectives.  Moreover, the data derived and reported allow for common understanding of progress and challenges to the training program, providing an opportunity for all stakeholders to prescriptively address issues before they impact compliance or readiness within the organization.

Methodology - Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative data collection consisted of open interviews with industry practitioners and corporate executive stakeholders in learning and development, coupled with open text response data in the survey. The interview protocol was implemented to collect impressions, opinions, and perceptions as they relate to the experience of a typical virtual live practitioner and learner, as well as relevant artifacts (policy documents, syllabi, learner notes, etc.) related to typical virtual live instructional experiences. 

During analysis of the data, a framework was used to develop the central concept and related concepts of a qualitative study, wherein the effectiveness of evaluation methodology is largely defined by both the outcome and the individual and his/her perception of the evaluation efficacy. Basic interpretive methodology was selected to allow the definition of evaluation terms and factors affecting them to emerge from the data collected. After initial coding, categories were axial coded along relational lines to develop and then refine themes that in turn were used to supplement the qualitative analysis of the survey data.

Limitations and Validity

In using directed (or purposeful) sampling for qualitative data collection, there is a potential to provide measures based on the particular affordances and perceptions of the learner within any given experience.  The experts solicited for interviews were carefully selected based on their expertise in both situated cognition theory and hybrid learning experience or need.  To maximize credibility of qualitative data, qualitative data was axially coded, member checked and compared with the results from an independent researcher coding the same data. 

For its part, online convenience sampling itself has been the subject of some research to identify any sampling biases and adverse respondent behaviors with mixed results, likely limiting generalizability and validity only to those in the qualified population of interest. As noted earlier, the evidence on the use of these sampling methods is mixed, but generally the threat of data validity through satisficing and similar sources of data inaccuracy have been found to be no worse than traditional methods.

Due to the limitations in this study,  the results (e.g., the respondent data and relationships) should be generalized with caution to other learner populations or situations beyond those conditions detailed in this and related studies performed by InSync. 
Title:	Summary

This research effort was undertaken to explore the current training evaluation practices in industry and identify both best practices and recommendations of the evaluation of training to answer that question.

Practitioners in organizational learning and development recognize that training is a resource intensive endeavor. As part of the intensive efforts to maintain workforce readiness and capability it is perhaps more important to take a step back and ask “what is this training meant to do?”.  The conclusions to be drawn from the data suggest that while evaluation is considered part of the instructional design, development, and implementation process, few organizations are implementing an evaluation approach that will permit them to answer the question with any certainty. 

Practitioners are encouraged to think about what data sources exist that might be leveraged as part of the evaluation approach – in this age of automated reporting and LMS-driven dashboards, some key relationships between short term feedback from learners and longer-term organizational outcomes might be available – most data collection is automated and can be collected with little effort. Irrespective of the evaluation framework in place, development of a meaningful logic model will suggest relationships and data sources that should be considered when implementing a training intervention. 

The characteristics of successful evaluation approaches can of course be adapted to the specific organizational needs to provide appropriate reporting – the key is the extensive relationships documented between the resources expended in training today and the organizational outcome seen in a year, or two. The capacity to identify with meaningful data that an individual with a particular training history is 30% more likely to retain, or that a department that received a particular training on development process showed a 12% increase in productivity  is valuable information – most organizations simply don’t have that information at hand. Wouldn’t it be great if they did.
