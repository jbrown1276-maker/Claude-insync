## 1. The report's spine (this is the argument to write toward)
ROI is a question about **cost and consequence**, so evaluation "must begin with the organizational objective rather than with data" (Executive Summary, L76). But the industry does the opposite: it starts from the data that is easy to collect (learner satisfaction) and never links it to the business goal. The result is a **wrong-question problem, not a bad-data problem.** Dye's foreword calls it "throwing expensive darts with a blindfold on" — you cannot tell if you hit the board (L58). And the kicker on ROI: you cannot claim a return when you never measured what was returned (L60).

**Conviction frame Sarah should lean on:** the principle isn't new. Kirkpatrick said in 1959 that evaluation should measure organizational impact; Phillips built ROI models around it; "the principle has been settled for six decades" (L54). What's new is the **scale of the failure to execute it** (L56). That is exactly September's "conviction, not re-teaching" posture: we are not discovering the gap, we are documenting how wide it is.

## 3. New angles the full read unlocked (Sarah didn't have these)
- **"One in eight."** A plainer way to say 13% for a leadership reader: of the teams using the industry-standard ROI frameworks, roughly one in eight actually collected data that reached organizational impact. (L60)
- **You can't claim ROI without measuring the return.** Dye's sharpest line, paraphrased. Strong for the reframe beat. (L60)
- **The 96 → 29 drop-off.** Structured training gets evaluated (96%); the messier, real-work training (on-the-job, 29%) barely does. Good for a practitioner-facing angle.
- **81% only use it to justify budget.** Evaluation has been reduced to a keep-or-cut lever, not a design tool. Sharp supporting stat for "the wrong question." (L104)
- **The aspirational close.** The report ends on what good looks like: knowing "an individual with a particular training history is 30% more likely to retain, or a department showed a 12% increase in productivity... most organizations simply don't have that information. Wouldn't it be great if they did." (L486) — a warm, forward note for a CTA or the post-webinar send. NB: the 30%/12% here are *illustrative examples in the report*, not survey findings — do not present them as InSync results.

## 4. Guardrails from the full read (do NOT do these)
- **Don't lead with the InSync solution.** The report's answer is an "expertise-modified CIPP, decision-oriented evaluation" with a logic model that starts from organizational objectives (L292). That is real and citable, but it is the *back* of the report and the *later* part of September. Week 1 stays on the diagnosis.
- **Don't overclaim representativeness.** Methodology is **convenience sampling** (Amazon Mechanical Turk + InSync's community of practice), with small financial incentives for MTurk respondents; the report itself says results "should be generalized with caution" (L339, L475, L477). Never imply a nationally representative or census-grade sample. "Industry survey of 200 practitioners who evaluate training at least quarterly" is the honest frame. Sample skews small-to-mid-sized, heavily regulated orgs (N=175 heavily regulated).
- **Sample is credible, not perfect** — median age 40.7 vs US workforce 41.8 supports reasonable diversity (L376). Fine to note the sample "mirrors the US workforce in age," not more.

## 5. One-line takeaway for the writing
The report doesn't argue that L&D collects bad data. It proves L&D is answering the wrong question — measuring what's easy (94% satisfaction) instead of what the business asked for (13% business outcomes) — and that the fix is to start from the organizational objective. September hands leaders that evidence, on the record.
