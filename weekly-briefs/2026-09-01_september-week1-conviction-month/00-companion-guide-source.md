INTRODUCTION: Why Guideposts Matter
Everyone knows training evaluation is important. Your executives know it. You know it. But knowing something matters and actually using it are two different things.
We surveyed 200 L&D leaders about their evaluation practices. Here is what they told us:
"A vast majority (83%) of respondents felt constrained by the evaluation data model in use and that the data itself provided an inaccurate or incomplete assessment of the training implementation. Respondent qualitative data revealed two main issues: The evaluation did not provide meaningful detail of the training and its outcomes (good or bad) or that the evaluation data provided no insight regarding the underlying organizational goals of the training."
Evaluation of Learning & Development: Industry Practices & Recommendations
The Three-Part Problem
Most L&D teams face three connected challenges:
Problem 1: We collect data and do nothing with it. Satisfaction scores get filed away. Completion rates go on a slide. The program stays the same next time.
Problem 2: For programs like leadership development, the actual business impact takes 12 to 18 months to show up. A CFO will not wait that long to fund next year's program.
Problem 3: Without evidence that tells us the program is working right now, we cannot improve it. We fly blind until it is too late.
The Guidepost Solution
This guide solves all three problems with one approach: building guideposts into your programs.
A guidepost is behavioral evidence you capture during or shortly after training that predicts the business outcome you care about. It tells you whether the program is working now, not months from now.
Instead of waiting 18 months to see if first-year manager retention improves, you have evidence after two to three weeks. Did managers apply the coaching framework in their first one-on-ones? Were they confident using it? Did their direct reports notice the change? Those are guideposts. They show the program is working before the business outcome shows up in the data.
How the 5 Shifts Solve These Problems
This guide uses the 5 Shifts framework to show you exactly how to build guideposts. Each shift solves at least one of the three problems.
Shift 1: From "what we delivered" to "what we promised" Solves Problem 1: Once you name the business outcome, you know exactly what data to collect and what to do with it.
Shift 2: From course completion to changed behavior Solves Problem 3: Observe behavior forming during the program. This is your first guidepost that the program is working now.
Shift 3: From measuring what is easy to measuring what they care about Solves Problem 2 and 3: Connect the behavior you observe to the business metric that actually matters. That connection bridges the time gap.
Shift 4: From the year-end autopsy to evidence along the way Solves Problem 2 and 3: Collect baseline, during, and after evidence. You have the story by week 4, not month 8.
Shift 5: From a data dump to a story leadership can repeat Solves Problem 1: Take one finding. Make one change. Measure it. Act on data, do not collect it.

SHIFT 1 — From "what we delivered" to "what we promised"
Solves Problem 1: Once you name the business outcome first, you know exactly what data to collect and what to do with it.
The Shift
Most measurement failures start with the wrong conversation, not bad data. Here is the pattern: The business asks L&D for a program. L&D builds it and delivers it. Then L&D tries to figure out whether it worked. The problem is nobody ever defined what "worked" means in words the business understands.
So L&D defaults to what it can control. How many people showed up. How many finished. Whether they liked it. In virtual delivery, this becomes session attendance, platform uptime, facilitator ratings. These metrics are easy to collect and simple to defend. They are also irrelevant to the person who controls your budget.
The shift is straightforward. Before you design anything, ask one question: What does success look like for this initiative, in words the business actually uses?
That is a design choice, not a measurement choice. When you design around a named business outcome, everything changes. The facilitator knows what to emphasize. The producer knows what to watch for. Breakout exercises target the right behavior. Follow-up points to transfer. Measurement does not start at the end. It starts here.
Why This Shift Helps You Build Guideposts
The guidepost is embedded in the outcome. Once you know what the business measures, you can identify signals that predict it. You stop guessing what to watch for. You know exactly what to look at because it is connected to the outcome from day one.
Shift 1's guidepost is a named business outcome that everyone aligns to. Everything that follows—design, delivery, observation, measurement—flows from this single point.

How to Adapt This Tool for Your Context
Designer perspective: You are not asking the business what content to build. You are asking what needs to be different as a result of the program. Push past "better skills." Find the business metric.
Stakeholder alignment: Get 30 minutes with the right person. If you cannot, that is a signal to wait. A misaligned outcome wastes everyone's time.
Program type considerations: Leadership outcomes often relate to retention, engagement, or behavior change. Technical skills often relate to error reduction or speed. Sales often relates to close rates or deal size. Let the function guide the language, but keep the business metric central.
Virtual, hybrid, or in-person: The modality changes how you design it, not what you are measuring. An in-person leadership program might emphasize peer relationships. Virtual might emphasize structured follow-up. The outcome stays the same.
What "good enough" looks like: One sentence. Simple words. Something the CEO could repeat without stumbling. Example: New managers will feel supported and confident in their first 90 days so they do not leave.
USE CASE SCENARIO: New Manager Onboarding Across 5 Regions
The situation: You are designing hybrid onboarding for newly promoted first-line managers across sales, operations, and customer service. The CEO told HR that first-year manager attrition is 34%, higher than industry benchmark. HR wants you to fix it. You have 4 weeks to design.
The trap: Defaulting to "train new managers on basic management skills." You could build a solid program on delegation, feedback, and goal-setting. If attrition is not the root cause of your outcome, the program will land perfectly and change nothing.
Using Shift 1: Before you design anything, sit with the regional heads and HR. Ask what success looks like 12 months from now. Do not let them say "better management." Dig. Is it retention? Productivity? Speed to independence? Push until they name the metric and the number. Once you have reduce attrition from 34% to under 20%, that is your North Star. Everything else flows from that.
The guidepost: You now design backward. You emphasize different content. You structure follow-up differently. You know what facilitators should watch for during practice. Measurement starts here, in design.
What good looks like: One sentence from the business stakeholder that does not contain the word "training." Example: New managers will have confidence and support in their first 90 days so attrition does not spike. Get it agreed to in writing. Share it with everyone designing and delivering.
Services at work: Design defines the program intent. Coaching or Train the Trainer works with facilitators on what to emphasize.

SHIFT 2 — From course completion to changed behavior
Solves Problem 3: Observe behavior forming during the program. This is your first guidepost that the program is working right now.
The Shift
A 98 percent completion rate tells you people finished. It tells you nothing about whether anyone does their job differently afterward. Your executives know this because they have sat through mandatory compliance training and know the difference between finishing and learning. When your main metric is one they already distrust, you confirm their suspicion that L&D does not understand measurement.
Live learning gives you something async cannot: you see behavior forming in real time. A facilitator watches whether participants apply concepts during practice. A producer tracks engagement in the room. Breakout exercises show whether people can use the framework, not just recall it. Chat shows whether thinking is shifting.
Most teams treat these signals as anecdotal. They are not. They are leading indicators of behavior change, captured in real time.
Draw a clear line: training to behavior to result. If you ran a sales training, what behaviors should change? If those behaviors change, what happens to close rates? That connection is credible measurement. Without it, you may have a design problem you have not found yet.
Why This Shift Helps You Build Guideposts
Behavioral observation during practice is the guidepost. If 80 percent of participants apply the framework correctly during a practice scenario in week 2, that is evidence the program is landing. You do not wait 3 months. You know in week 2 whether to adjust for the next cohort.
Shift 2's guidepost is behavioral observation. Does the behavior appear during practice the way you designed it to? That tells you everything about whether the design is working.

Dedicated producer versus facilitator alone: With a producer, they handle observation. Brief them on 2 to 3 behaviors to watch. Without a producer, give the facilitator a one-page template to fill out after breakouts, not during. Do not add work that gets in the way of delivery.

USE CASE SCENARIO: Sales Training Launch—Can They Actually Do It?
The situation: You designed a hybrid sales process training that launches in 3 weeks. First cohort is 40 reps across 2 regional offices. Your VP wants to know whether the training changes how reps sell. You have a facilitator and a producer for each region.
The trap: Waiting 6 weeks for survey data and hoping it says yes. Missing 3 weeks of live observation where you can actually see whether reps apply the framework or revert to old habits.
Using Shift 2: During live modules, build in practice scenarios. Real sales calls. Real objections. Real discovery conversations. Brief the facilitator and producer: Watch for this. Are they asking discovery questions or jumping to solutions? During breakouts, they note which reps demonstrate the behavior and which struggle. If 80 percent nailed it, the program is landing. If 30 percent did, you have a design problem to fix for cohort 2.
The guidepost: Two weeks after training, ask reps a quick 2-question follow-up: Did you use the discovery framework? Yes or no. If yes, what happened in the call? You are asking whether the behavior changed, not whether they liked it. Connect it to what the facilitator observed. If 75 percent reported using it and 80 percent showed the behavior during practice, the program works.

Services at work: Facilitation and Production capture behavioral data in real time. Design creates practice moments that allow observation.

SHIFT 3 — From measuring what is easy to measuring what they care about
Solves Problem 2 and 3: Connect the behavior you observe to the business metric that matters. That connection bridges the time gap and shows the program is working.
The Shift
We measure satisfaction because it is simple and makes us look good. A 4.7 out of 5 on a survey feels like evidence. No CFO has ever protected a training budget because the smile sheets came back warm.
The instinct to measure what is easy is not laziness. L&D teams are understaffed and grab what is available. The LMS spits out completion data. A survey captures reactions. These numbers go on a slide and everyone pretends that is enough.
But if you are delivering live virtual or hybrid learning, you are already sitting on richer data. Your platform captures polls, chat frequency, breakout participation, hand raises, and engagement signals. The data is there. The gap is knowing which signals connect to outcomes your business actually cares about.
Choose a small set of leading and lagging indicators tied to the outcome you named in Shift 1. Leading indicators show whether the right behaviors are forming during the program. Lagging indicators show whether those behaviors produced the result the business cares about afterward.
This is the uncomfortable part. The metrics that earn trust often live outside your department. Sales data lives in the CRM. Customer satisfaction lives with service. Employee retention lives in HR. You need to build relationships with the people who own those numbers. That cross-functional work is why most L&D teams avoid it. It is also why teams that do it earn credibility. When you show up with data from the business's own systems, you are not asking them to trust your numbers. You are showing them theirs.
Why This Shift Helps You Build Guideposts
When you know which business metrics matter and what behaviors predict them, you build a chain of evidence. Observation during training (leading) to transfer after training (intermediate) to business metric change (lagging). Each point in that chain is a guidepost.
Shift 3's guidepost is the connection itself: behavior during training predicts business outcome after. That connection is what turns observation into proof.

Leading indicator (what you observe): Choose a behavior that is observable and hard to fake. For coaching, the questions asked. For presentations, audience engagement created. For customer service, empathy shown. For technical training, step completion accuracy.

USE CASE SCENARIO: Customer Service Training—What Actually Matters?
The situation: Your customer service team launched a new conflict de-escalation framework. You trained 60 reps across 3 shifts in hybrid delivery. Your director wants to know if it worked. You can measure satisfaction, completion rates, and rep confidence. But what does your director actually care about?
The trap: Reporting that 95 percent felt confident and 92 percent completed the training. Director nods politely. Then you discover customer complaints have not dropped, handle time has not improved, and satisfaction scores have not moved. The data you collected was irrelevant.
Using Shift 3: Before you collect anything, ask what the business actually measures. For customer service: CSAT, first-contact resolution rate, customer effort score, handle time. Your director owns or accesses this data in the contact center system. Work backward. What behavior during training predicts these outcomes? Reps showing empathy and curiosity during practice (leading) are more likely to de-escalate real calls without escalation (lagging, measurable in the system).
The guidepost: You observe during practice: Did reps use the de-escalation framework with genuine curiosity or did they rush through it? Two weeks after training, your director pulls CSAT scores for trained reps versus untrained. Better CSAT means the program works. No difference means design problem. You have intermediate proof weeks in, not months out.
What good looks like: A two-column worksheet. Left: What we will observe during training (genuine empathy, active listening, follow-up questions). Right: What the business measures (CSAT, FCR rate, handle time). Fill in the connection. That is your measurement map.

SHIFT 4 — From the year-end autopsy to evidence along the way
Solves Problem 2 and 3: Collect baseline, during, and after evidence. You have the story by week 4, not month 8. You can improve based on actual data, not hope.
The Shift
Most measurement happens after the program is over. Someone remembers leadership asked for impact data, so the team scrambles to find whatever numbers exist. By then it is too late to prove anything or improve anything. You are writing a post-mortem for a program already judged.
The teams executives believe are the ones gathering evidence before, during, and after. The story writes itself.
Before means establishing a baseline. What does your target metric look like right now, before the program launches? If you are trying to reduce new-hire ramp time, know what ramp time is today. Without a baseline, any post-program number floats in space.
During is where live learning has a real advantage. Every session is an observation point. A facilitator notes where participants struggle with application. A producer documents engagement patterns. When you build observation into the producer role, evidence capture becomes part of delivery, not a separate task. If you have no producer, the facilitator uses a simple template filled out after breakouts.
After means following up when the program ends, not six months later but two to three weeks later and again at six weeks, with specific questions tied to the behaviors you named in Shift 2. Did the manager use the coaching framework? Did the sales team adjust their discovery process?
None of this is complex evaluation theory. It is project management. Plan what you need to know, build collection into your timeline, and stop treating evidence as something you hunt for when the executive review is already scheduled.
Why This Shift Helps You Build Guideposts
When you collect before, during, and after, you have evidence by week 4, not month 8. You have leading indicators (observation), transfer data (behavior change), and a baseline to compare. That is intermediate proof. That is guideposts.
Shift 4's guidepost is continuous evidence collection. You are not waiting for a final story. You are building it week by week.

How to Adapt This Tool for Your Context
Establishing baseline: Ask: What data exists now that measures the outcome I care about? If it is retention, ask HR when they can pull historical rates. If it is CSAT, ask the service leader when they can share historical scores. Do not make up data. Use what already exists.
During delivery: Ask: What can facilitators and producers realistically capture without disrupting delivery? If they are overwhelmed facilitating, do not add observation. If you have a dedicated producer, they can capture more. Be honest about capacity.

USE CASE SCENARIO: Multi-Cohort Facilitation Program—Building Evidence Continuously
The situation: You are running a 4-week hybrid coaching skills program. You will deliver it 6 times over 6 months (one cohort per month, 30 people per cohort). Your VP wants proof it is working. Final impact data (manager retention, team engagement) will not arrive until month 8. What do you do in months 1 through 7?
The trap: Waiting until month 8 to gather all data. Discovering mid-way through month 3 that the program is not landing but you have already run 3 cohorts the same way. Or collecting completion and satisfaction for all 6 cohorts and having nothing actionable.
Using Shift 4: BEFORE launch (month 0): Establish baseline. What do manager retention and team engagement look like today? Document them. DURING each delivery: Producer captures observations. What coaching behaviors showed up in practice? Facilitator documents engagement. This is part of delivery. AFTER each cohort (weeks 2-3): Quick follow-up. Have you used the coaching framework? What happened? By month 3, you have data from 3 cohorts: Framework adoption is at 65 percent. Facilitators saw stronger application in Cohorts 1 and 2 but weaker in Cohort 3 (design issue).
The guidepost: You do not wait 6 months. You have mid-program evidence after Cohort 2 that tells you what to adjust for Cohort 3. By Cohort 4, you have made a redesign based on data. By Cohort 6, you have 4 months of adoption data showing the trend toward your goal (improved retention).
What good looks like: A simple calendar. Cohort 1 (Weeks 1-4), observation checklist filled (Week 4), 2-week follow-up (Week 6), Cohort 2 launches (Week 7), adjustment made based on Cohort 1 data, repeat. The story is written by month 4, not month 8.

SHIFT 5 — From a data dump to a story leadership can repeat
Solves Problem 1: Take one finding. Make one change. Measure it. Act on data instead of collecting it and filing it away.
The Shift
You have done the work. You named the outcome, connected it to behavior, chose real metrics, and gathered evidence along the way. Now comes the part where most L&D teams stumble at the finish.
They build a 40-slide deck packed with charts, methodology notes, and caveats. Executives tune out by slide 8 because the presentation answers a question nobody asked with detail nobody wanted.
Your executives do not need your dashboard. They need one clear sentence they can repeat.
That is the real test. Not whether you have data, but whether you can translate it into a business narrative simple enough to travel without you. When your VP of Sales tells the board, "The new onboarding program cut ramp time by three weeks and the Q2 cohort is outperforming Q1," that is measurement working. It works because the story lives in someone else's mouth, not your spreadsheet.
One page. Before state. Behavior change. Business result. If you cannot fit your story on one page, you are reporting data, not telling a narrative. Data without narrative is noise that makes busy people feel more busy.
Why This Shift Helps You Build Guideposts
Shift 5 closes the loop. When you translate data into one clear design change for the next cohort, you prove the measurement system works. You are not just collecting data. You are acting on it. That is the guidepost that matters most: You saw the issue, you fixed it, and the next cohort is better.
Shift 5's guidepost is action. You move from data to decision. The next cohort reflects what you learned. That is proof the system works.
TOOL: Finding-to-Redesign One-Pager
Use this to turn your data into one clear decision. Literally one page.
Section
What to Write
FINDING
One sentence about what the data shows. Example: 32 percent of Cohort 2 did not use the framework. Of those, 87 percent said they did not feel confident in the moment.
REDESIGN
One change you are making for the next cohort. Example: For Cohort 3, we are adding 15 minutes of extra scenario practice and a one-page job aid.
EXPECTED OUTCOME
What you expect to change. Example: Adoption rate moves from 68 percent to 75 percent or higher. Confidence barrier reduced by 20 percent.
How to Adapt This Tool for Your Context
Translating different data types: Observation data: 75 percent of participants applied the framework correctly in practice. Transfer data: 62 percent reported using it on the job. Business metric data: Manager retention in the program cohort was 18 percent versus 34 percent company average. Combine these for one story.
One decision focus: Pick the ONE finding that would move the needle most if fixed. Do not overhaul the program. One thing. Add a job aid. Extend practice time. Change the facilitator emphasis. One change. Measure it. Then, if needed, address the next thing.
Variations by audience: For your director: Focus on the finding and the fix. For the CEO: Focus on the business impact. For the facilitator: Focus on how to implement the change. Match the audience.
How to present: Email? One paragraph. Verbal debrief? Two minutes. Slide deck? One slide. Do not create extra work. Your data is strong because you act on it fast, not because you document it exhaustively.
Setting expectations: Communicate clearly: We saw this in Cohort 2. We are trying this in Cohort 3. We will know if it worked by end of Cohort 3. Then tell them the results. That is iterative improvement. That is how you build trust.
USE CASE SCENARIO: You've Collected Data. Now What?
The situation: You finished Cohort 2 of your leadership onboarding program (50 new managers trained). You have facilitator observation notes (some struggled with practice scenarios, others nailed it), 2-week follow-up survey (68 percent reported using the framework, 32 percent said forgot or did not feel confident). Your director wants a report. You have a folder of data. What do you tell them? What do you change for Cohort 3?
The trap: Writing a 12-slide report with charts, methodology, caveats, completion rates, satisfaction, and observation data. Director reads slides 1 and 2, sees charts, asks so what are we changing, and you realize you do not have a clear answer.
Using Shift 5: Pick the ONE finding that explains why 32 percent did not use the framework. Look at the data: Of the 16 managers who said they did not use it, 14 mentioned did not feel confident in the moment and 2 mentioned forgot the steps. That is your finding. ONE clear reason. Make ONE redesign decision: For Cohort 3, we are adding 15 minutes of extra scenario practice and a one-page job aid. Not overhauling the program. One thing.
The guidepost: Your director emails: Got it. You are seeing confidence as the barrier. Extra reps and job aid for C3. When can you tell me if it moves the needle? Month 4, after Cohort 3: Yes. Usage went from 68 percent to 81 percent. The job aid helped. Confidence is still the barrier for 8 managers but better.
What good looks like: A one-page document. TOP: Finding: 32 percent of Cohort 2 did not use the framework. Root cause: 87 percent felt not confident in the moment. MIDDLE: Redesign for Cohort 3: Add 15-minute practice scenario and one-page job aid. BOTTOM: Expected outcome: adoption rate moves from 68 percent to 75 percent or higher. That is the document your director reads and acts on.
Services at work: Evaluation synthesizes data. Design translates findings into action. Coaching or Train the Trainer may support facilitator adaptation.

8-WEEK PROGRESS MONITOR
This is not a detailed project plan. It is a checkpoint guide. By each milestone, you should see evidence that the measurement system is working.
Week 1-2: Outcome Guidepost Locked
Check:
You have one clear statement of business outcome from the stakeholder
It is in words the business uses, not training language
Everyone designing and delivering knows it
You have documented what success looks like in terms the business cares about
Week 2-3: Behavioral Guidepost Identified
Check:
You know what behavior you are watching for during practice
You have identified 1-2 observation moments during the program
You know what question you will ask during follow-up
You have mapped this behavior to the business metric it predicts
Week 4-5: Observation Guidepost Captured
Check:
Facilitator or producer captured observations during practice moments
You have notes on what you saw. Which participants applied the framework? Which struggled?
You know whether the observation matched your expectations
You are noticing patterns that might change how you deliver next time
Week 6-7: Transfer Guidepost Confirmed
Check:
You have 2-3 week follow-up data from participants
You can answer: Did they use it? Did the behavior stick?
You have one clear finding. One reason things worked or did not work
You can see the connection between what you observed and what they reported
Week 8: Redesign Guidepost Planned
Check:
You have one clear redesign decision for the next cohort
It is tied directly to the finding, not a guess
You have communicated it to leadership with expected outcome
You know what to measure to see if the change works
If you cannot check these boxes, that is not failure. It is information. It tells you where to focus.

CONCLUSION: From Guideposts to Impact
You do not need perfect conditions. You do not need a year-long plan. You need one clear behavior, one observation moment, one follow-up, and one design decision. That is evaluation that works.
The research showed that most organizations feel constrained by their evaluation practice. You do not have to be. By evaluating less but more intentionally, you will have stronger data and better programs.
