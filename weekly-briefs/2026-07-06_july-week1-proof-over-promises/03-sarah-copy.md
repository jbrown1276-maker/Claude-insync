## DELIVERABLE 1: Learning Leadership Series Article — Jennifer Hofmann

**Title:** When Live Learning Is Worth the Investment

---

There's a particular moment I've been in more times than I can count. The program wrapped well. The facilitators were prepared. Participants engaged. And then, a few weeks later, someone in a budget review asks the one question the data can't answer: *Did it actually change anything?*

You pull up the evaluation report. Completion: 94%. Satisfaction: 4.2 out of 5. And you watch the room go quiet in a way that has nothing to do with the numbers being bad.

The numbers just don't answer the question.

I've spent decades working with organizations to design and deliver live learning — virtual classrooms, instructor-led programs, blended formats. In that time, I've learned that the measurement problem most teams are sitting with isn't a metrics problem. It's a structural one. And until we name it clearly, we keep solving the wrong thing.

**What the standard dashboard is actually measuring**

Most post-program reports tell you two things: who showed up, and whether they liked it. These are not useless numbers — they tell you something real about logistics and learner experience. But they don't tell you whether the learning changed behavior. They don't tell you whether the program was worth what it cost.

That gap — between the data you have and the question being asked of you — is not a failure of execution. It's a consequence of how most measurement frameworks were designed. They were built to track activity. They were not built to track impact.

This distinction matters, because the pressure to demonstrate impact isn't going away. According to LEO Learning and Watershed, 70% of L&D leaders report being under increasing pressure to prove the value of their programs. Only 48% measure anything beyond completion. Those two numbers describe the gap exactly: nearly everyone is being asked to answer a question their current measurement approach cannot answer.

**Why it's structural, not personal**

I want to be direct about this, because I've watched skilled practitioners take this personally. If your dashboard isn't answering the CFO's question, it's almost certainly not because you chose the wrong KPIs or failed to set up the right survey. It's because measuring the impact of live learning requires capturing something that happens over time — and most evaluation frameworks aren't designed to do that.

Live learning is a sequence of events, not a single event. Something happens before the session. Something happens during it. And something — or nothing — happens afterward, in the 30, 60, 90 days when the learning either gets applied or quietly disappears. The completion rate only tells you about one of those moments.

**A frame that already exists**

There's a way to think about this that I've found useful — not as a theoretical model, but as a practical checklist for asking better questions.

Live learning that produces measurable outcomes tends to get three conditions right. First, there is design intent: the program was built with a specific behavioral outcome in mind, not just content coverage. Second, there is delivery reliability: the session itself was facilitated in a way that prepared participants to apply what they learned, not just absorb it. Third, there is learning continuity: something was in place — manager conversations, application exercises, check-ins — to support transfer in the 30, 60, and 90 days after the session ended.

When all three conditions are met, the measurement story changes. You have something to point to at each stage. You're not reporting into a void.

When one of them is missing — when the design didn't specify a target behavior, or when nothing was in place after the session ended — the gap isn't a surprise. It was baked in.

**The question you can ask differently now**

Most post-program reviews ask: "How did it go?" That question gets you satisfaction scores.

A more useful question is: "What were the conditions we built for?" Did we design for a specific behavior change? Did the delivery support application, not just attention? Is there anything in place in the next 90 days that would allow the learning to stick?

If the answer to any of those is "we didn't think about that," that's not a failure — that's a starting point. It tells you where the measurement gap actually lives.

**Where to go from here**

If this frame is useful, there's a more complete version of it worth reading. The Live Learning Formula — A Strategic Guide to Making Live Learning Work outlines all three conditions in depth, with case study evidence from organizations that have applied this approach at scale. It's the kind of document worth having before a budget conversation, not after one.

Each email assumes the reader has seen earlier touches — none restates the problem from scratch, but none requires the previous emails to make sense on its own.
